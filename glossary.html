Glossary

The daily analysis assumes some fluency with operational meteorology. This page unpacks the agencies, charts, concepts, units, and acronyms that appear most often.

Agencies & Products

NOAA
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration — the US agency whose centers produce most of the data Tlaloc reads.
NWS
National Weather Service, the NOAA branch responsible for public forecasts and warnings. Its national centers each own a specialty below.
WPC
Weather Prediction Center. Draws the official US surface analysis and writes the short-range (days 1–2) and extended (days 3–7) forecast discussions, with emphasis on heavy rain and winter weather.
SPC
Storm Prediction Center. Owns severe-thunderstorm and tornado forecasting: the daily convective outlooks, mesoscale discussions, and severe-weather watches.
Convective outlook categories
SPC ranks severe-thunderstorm risk in five steps: Marginal (MRGL), Slight (SLGT), Enhanced (ENH), Moderate (MDT), and High (HIGH). “Slight” is already a day worth watching; Moderate and High days are rare and serious.
Mesoscale Discussion (MCD)
A short SPC bulletin issued while forecasters actively monitor a developing regional threat — often the step before a watch is issued. MCDs exist only when something is happening, so “no active MCDs” is itself useful information.
NHC
National Hurricane Center. Issues the Tropical Weather Outlooks (TWOs) for the Atlantic and East Pacific basins, giving each disturbance a formation probability through 48 hours and 7 days.
CPC
Climate Prediction Center. Covers the beyond-a-week timescale: the 6–10 and 8–14 day outlooks and the official ENSO monitoring, including the ONI table Tlaloc reads.
NESDIS / STAR
The NOAA satellite service and its science branch, which publish the GOES imagery Tlaloc uses.
ECCC
Environment and Climate Change Canada, whose Canadian Meteorological Centre draws an independent surface analysis of North America with detailed frontal structure over Canada.
SMN
Servicio Meteorológico Nacional, Mexico's national weather service (under CONAGUA, the national water commission).
College of DuPage (COD) NEXLAB
A university weather lab whose site republishes analysis charts — including the 500 mb chart Tlaloc reads — in a clean, consistent form.

Charts & Imagery

Surface analysis
A hand-analyzed snapshot of conditions at ground level: high- and low-pressure centers with central pressures, fronts, and isobars. It is an analysis of what is, not a forecast.
Isobar
A line of constant surface pressure. Tightly packed isobars mean a strong pressure gradient and wind; widely spaced (“slack”) isobars mean light winds.
Fronts
Boundaries between air masses. Cold fronts (blue triangles) mark advancing cold air; warm fronts (red half-circles) advancing warm air; a stationary front (alternating symbols) isn't moving — a common focus for repeated rounds of rain; an occluded front (purple) marks a mature cyclone where the cold front has caught the warm front.
500 mb chart
A map of the atmosphere at the 500-millibar pressure level, roughly 5.5–6 km up — near the level that steers surface weather. The contours are geopotential heights: where the 500 mb surface sits high (ridges, warm), and where it dips (troughs, cold).
Geopotential height / dam
The altitude of a pressure surface, given in decameters (dam; 1 dam = 10 m). “5800 dam at 500 mb” describes a very warm, strong summer ridge.
Air Mass RGB
A composite satellite product built from infrared and water-vapor channels, color-coded by air mass: reds/oranges show dry, high-PV stratospheric intrusions; greens tropical moist air; dark blues cold, dry upper-tropospheric air; bright white high, cold cloud tops (deep convection).
GOES East / GOES West
NOAA's two operational geostationary satellites, parked over the Americas' east and west flanks. Tlaloc uses GOES East, in both its CONUS sector and the Full Disk view that spans Canada, Mexico, Central America, and both tropical basins.
CONUS / Full Disk
Satellite sector names: CONUS is the contiguous United States frame; Full Disk is the entire Earth face visible to the satellite.

Synoptic Concepts

Synoptic scale
The scale of whole weather systems — roughly 1000 km and up, the size of cyclones, ridges, and troughs. “Mesoscale” is the next size down: individual storm complexes, squall lines, sea breezes.
Trough / ridge
The waves in the upper-level flow. A trough is a southward dip (low heights, cold, stormy); a ridge is a northward bulge (high heights, warm, quiet). The alternation of troughs and ridges is the day's pattern.
Longwave vs. shortwave
Longwaves are the continent-scale waves that define the regime and move slowly; shortwaves are smaller ripples embedded in them that often trigger individual storm systems.
Trough tilt (positive / neutral / negative)
The orientation of a trough axis. A negatively tilted trough (leaning northwest–southeast) is often the most dynamic configuration, favoring rapid storm development and severe weather.
Cutoff low
A low that has pinched off from the main westerly flow and circulates on its own. With no steering current, cutoffs drift slowly and can repeat rain over the same area for days.
Blocking
A stalled configuration — often a strong ridge — that stops the usual west-to-east progression. Under a block, extremes compound: heat builds under the ridge, and rain repeats near any trapped low.
Rex block / omega block
The two classic block geometries, and both are specific. A Rex block stacks a high directly poleward of a cutoff low at roughly the same longitude; an omega block sets a ridge between two lows, tracing the Greek letter. A broad western ridge with a separate low far to the east is blocked flow, but it is neither of these — it is better described as an amplified ridge–cutoff dipole.
Height anomaly
A 500 mb height compared against its climatological normal, rather than in absolute dam. This is the distinction that decides whether something is merely high or genuinely unusual: 594 dm is a strong ridge in January and ordinary in July. The same caution applies to temperature — an absolute 500 mb reading is “unseasonably cool,” not “anomalous,” unless an anomaly product says so.
Ensemble mean
The average of many model runs started from slightly different initial states. Individual runs produce dramatic features that average out if the ensemble doesn’t agree, so the mean is the honest test of whether a pattern change is real — a single striking forecast is much less persuasive than a mean that shifts.
Ridge regeneration
Whether a ridge rebuilds behind the disturbance that flattened it. A cold front crossing a heat dome guarantees a cool-down; it does not guarantee a regime change. If the ensemble-mean height anomaly returns to the same place a few days later, the block was dented, not broken — and blocks often contract geographically (retreating toward the Southwest) rather than ending.
Jet stream / jet streak
The ribbon of strong upper-level winds along air-mass boundaries. A jet streak is a local wind maximum within it; air accelerating into and out of a streak creates the lift that spins up surface storms.
Cyclogenesis
The birth or intensification of a surface low-pressure system, typically downstream of an approaching upper-level trough.
PV / stratospheric intrusion
Potential vorticity — a marker of stratospheric air. Where dry, high-PV air folds down toward the troposphere (vivid red in Air Mass RGB imagery), the upper atmosphere is dynamically active, often alongside a strong jet and developing storms.
CAPE
Convective Available Potential Energy (J/kg) — the fuel gauge for thunderstorms. A few hundred is marginal; 1000+ supports strong storms; 3000+ is a loaded environment.
Wind shear
Change of wind speed or direction with height. Shear organizes storms: with ample CAPE, strong deep-layer shear favors supercells and severe weather.
Helicity (SRH)
Storm-relative helicity measures low-level wind spiraling available to rotating storms; higher values (m²/s²) mean better odds a storm can rotate and produce a tornado.

Units & Notation

mb / hPa
Millibars and hectopascals — identical units of pressure (1 mb = 1 hPa). Sea-level pressure averages ~1013 mb; a deep winter storm can dip below 970 mb; a strong high can top 1040 mb.
dam
Decameter, 10 meters; the unit for geopotential heights.
kt
Knot, one nautical mile per hour (≈1.15 mph, ≈1.85 km/h).
UTC / Z
Coordinated Universal Time, meteorology's common clock; “12Z” means 12:00 UTC. Upper-air analyses are made twice daily, at 00Z and 12Z.

Climate & Seasonal Context

ENSO
El Niño–Southern Oscillation — the slow seesaw of tropical Pacific ocean temperatures that tilts the odds in North American seasonal weather. El Niño is the warm phase, La Niña the cool phase.
ONI
Oceanic Niño Index — the running three-month average of sea-surface temperature anomalies in the tropical Pacific's Niño 3.4 region. Values ≥ +0.5 °C indicate El Niño conditions, ≤ −0.5 °C La Niña.
Teleconnection index
A single number summarizing how strongly the atmosphere resembles a recurring large-scale pattern, in standard deviations from normal. Convenient but lossy: an index is a projection of the height field, so it is a cross-check on a story the charts already tell, never the basis for one. The trend usually matters more than the value.
PNA
Pacific/North American pattern. Strongly positive favors ridging over western North America with troughing in the East — the summer heat-dome signature. A drift toward neutral or negative is one hint that an amplified western ridge is relaxing.
AO / NAO / EPO
The Arctic and North Atlantic Oscillations and the East Pacific Oscillation. Negative AO and EPO favor high-latitude blocking; the NAO bears on whether a cutoff low over the eastern US can escape out to sea or stalls.
North American Monsoon
The summer wind shift that draws tropical moisture into Mexico and the US Southwest, fueling near-daily afternoon thunderstorms from roughly late June through September.
Bermuda High
The semi-permanent subtropical Atlantic high. In summer its western flank pumps humid Gulf air northward into the central and eastern US — the classic source of muggy heat and storm fuel.
Hurricane season
Atlantic: June 1 – November 30, peaking around September 10. East Pacific: May 15 – November 30, often busiest in July–August.
HeatRisk
An NWS index (Minor → Moderate → Major → Extreme) rating how unusual and health-threatening a stretch of heat is for a location and date.

Named for Tláloc, the Mexica deity of water, rain, fertility, and storms.
