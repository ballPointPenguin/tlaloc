Synoptic Picture

Blocked ridge-cutoff dipole holds into a fourth day: Labor Day heat dome anchors the midsection while a deepening southeast-Canada trough drives Great Lakes-to-Mid-Atlantic severe and a Northeast cool-down

The high-amplitude, blocked longwave pattern Tlaloc has tracked all week is still the organizing story, now in its fourth-plus day. The DuPage 500 mb analysis reads the same three-part structure: a cutoff low (560-564 dam) over the Pacific Northwest and interior British Columbia, a strongly amplified ridge (588-594 dam) arcing from the central Rockies and southern Plains into the Great Lakes and Ontario, and an eastern trough (570-576 dam) over the Ohio Valley and Mid-Atlantic. As the summaries correctly note, the western anticyclone sits equatorward and east of the cutoff rather than poleward of it, so this is an amplified ridge-cutoff dipole, not a true Rex block. The flow is decidedly blocked rather than progressive, and the practical consequence is a heat dome anchored over the midsection through the Labor Day weekend — WPC advertises major to extreme heat with widespread Heat Advisories and Extreme Heat Warnings — with the action, as usual, living on the flanks of the ridge.

Today's focal points line up on those flanks. The severe threat is a Slight Risk from the southern Great Lakes through the Mid-Atlantic, tied to a cluster/MCS moving southeast out of the upper Midwest into the diurnally destabilizing Ohio Valley; damaging outflow winds are the primary concern, with the best overlap of shear and buoyancy over central and eastern Ohio and bowing segments expected. This is driven by the upper trough deepening over southeastern Canada, which is also steering a genuine cool air mass into the Northeast — temperatures dropping into the 40s-50s by Sunday morning. The heavy-rain headlines are secondary but real: monsoonal moisture over Arizona and New Mexico carries a Slight Risk of excessive rainfall along the AZ-NM border, and weak disturbances keep the Gulf Coast and Florida vulnerable to flash flooding within the deep tropical moisture that the Air Mass RGB shows blanketing the entire Southeast and Gulf. In Canada, the western baroclinic zone has shifted north relative to yesterday: the deep BC/Yukon low has retreated toward the Alaska-Yukon border (about 986 hPa) and a stout 1031 hPa high has built over interior western Canada, tightening the gradient and focusing lift across the Prairie provinces on the high's northern and western flanks.

The upper-level context ties it together and answers the regime question. The broad red-orange plume on the Air Mass RGB from the central Rockies across the southern Plains into the lower Mississippi Valley is warm, dry, low-PV air on the anticyclonic side of the ridge — the heat and convective suppression there come from subsidence, midlevel warming, and capping, not from the modest surface highs beneath. The sharp orange-to-green transition running from the Texas Panhandle toward the Ohio Valley marks the jet corridor and airmass boundary, and impulses along it feed the northern-stream severe. On whether the southeast-Canada trough is disrupting the heat regime: the ensemble pair says no — this is geographic contraction, not collapse. The Day 2 outlook confirms the eastern shortwave is progressive, sweeping the severe threat southeast to the central Appalachians and Carolinas Saturday behind a southward-advancing front while substantial ridging still sits over the Plains. Comparing the 6-10 day (Sep 9-13) to the 8-14 day (Sep 11-17), the western ridge keeps retreating: the zero anomaly line already lies near the Pacific coast at 6-10 days with more zonal flow offshore, and by 8-14 days there is no positive-anomaly ridge over the northern Rockies or northern Plains at all — the ridge axis has slid to the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico (576-582 dam) with only a weak downstream bump over Kansas-Oklahoma, all while a deep polar low anchors the Canadian Arctic. That is the classic ridge-retreat-toward-the-Southwest signature: the southern tier stays hot, the northern tier trends cooler and more active. The weakly negative and falling PNA and the positive, rising AO are directionally consistent with fading western ridging and reduced high-latitude blocking, but as weak summer signals they serve only as a lagging cross-check on the height and PV fields.

Climate Context

This is peak-season North America running close to script for early September under a developing El Niño. The ONI table shows the basin having warmed from weak La Niña (about -0.6 C late 2025) to moderate El Niño by summer 2026 (+1.80 in JJA), and the tropics fit that state precisely: a crowded East Pacific carrying three simultaneous hurricanes (Karina, Lowell, Marie) plus a 50 percent chance of another system developing well offshore southwestern Mexico next week, set against a suppressed Atlantic where — at the climatological hurricane-season peak — no development is expected anywhere for seven days. The North American Monsoon remains active, driving daily Southwest and high-terrain convection with the excessive-rainfall risk along the AZ-NM border. Severe weather is expressing itself in the expected late-summer mode — ridge-periphery, northern-stream and frontal storms across the Great Lakes, Ohio Valley, and Appalachians rather than deep-Plains outbreaks — while the dominant domestic climate signal remains the durable, dangerous central-U.S. heat now peaking over the Labor Day weekend.
