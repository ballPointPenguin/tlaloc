Synoptic Picture

Nor'easter deepens off the Mid-Atlantic while the western trough closes into a Great Basin cutoff — and the ensembles now flatten the mid-latitude West rather than rebuilding the ridge, with amplification retreating to the Canadian Arctic

The fall longwave dipole that has organized the continent all week is still in place, but both ends have matured. At the eastern end, the coastal cyclone tracked for several days has deepened further — now near 986 mb off the Mid-Atlantic, down from 996 mb a day ago — and remains coupled to a negatively tilted 500 mb shortwave curving through the Ohio Valley and Mid-Atlantic. With the NAO sharply negative (7-day mean near -1.01), this is a trapped, stalling system rather than a progressive one, and WPC expects it to linger off the Northeast before exiting toward the Canadian Maritimes by midweek. At the western end, the deep Pacific Northwest/BC trough of recent days has done what a blocked pattern does: it has detached into a genuine cutoff low over the central Great Basin, with an innermost contour near 564 dam and a cold core of -12 to -14 C over Nevada and Utah, while the ridge has rebuilt to its northwest across the Northwest and western Canada. The geometry — anticyclone displaced northwest of the low rather than stacked poleward of it — is a classically amplified western ridge paired with a cutoff, not a Rex block. Between the two, deep tropical moisture floods the Gulf and Southeast, and a cold front trails from a weak Iowa/Missouri low south into Texas.

Today's centers of action: first, the Nor'easter, driving major multi-cycle coastal flooding, gale-force gusts of 45-65 mph over eastern Long Island and southeastern New England, heavy rain, and a WPC Slight Risk for excessive rainfall over southern New England. Second, the Great Basin cutoff, which will pivot slowly before ejecting east into the Plains midweek; ahead of it, tropical moisture drawn from East Pacific Hurricane Polo sets up a multi-day heavy-rain and flash-flood regime spreading from the Four Corners across the Plains and Mississippi Valley, with mountain snow in the higher terrain — WPC already hangs broad Marginal and embedded Slight Risk excessive-rainfall areas through midweek. Third, Canada carries real weight today: a deep 975 hPa low over the Gulf of Alaska drives a tight wind gradient along the BC coast, while a 1037 hPa high over Hudson Bay reinforces unseasonably cool air across the Prairie provinces, and a separate 987 hPa low near Labrador trails fronts toward the Northeast. In Mexico, Hurricane Polo near Cabo Corrientes is both a direct coastal-impact threat and the moisture source for the Four Corners rains.

Upstairs, the Air Mass RGB ties the eastern story together cleanly: a vivid, compact high-PV dry streamer wraps cyclonically around the Mid-Atlantic cutoff — a credible stratospheric-intrusion signature on its cyclonic flank — while the sharpest airmass boundary, marking the implied jet corridor, runs from the central Plains northeast through the Ohio Valley, separating dry upper air to the northwest from deep green tropical moisture to the southeast. The regime-change test is where today departs from recent framing. Comparing the two ensemble leads directly, both the 6-10 day (Oct 1-5) and 8-14 day (Oct 3-9) means put their dominant positive height anomaly over the Canadian Arctic near Baffin (+6 to +9 dam), not over the western or south-central U.S. The 6-10 day flattens the CONUS toward zonal flow with the zero-anomaly line cutting through the Pacific Northwest and no positive dome over the West; the 8-14 day shows a ridge axis arcing from the Southwest into the Arctic but with western-U.S. anomalies near zero and a deepening east Pacific trough (-6 to -12 dam west of BC). WPC's extended discussion reinforces this, noting guidance has trended toward the lower-amplitude, more split-flow ECMWF camp. In other words, the mid-latitude western ridge is genuinely not regenerating over the lower 48 — the durable, self-repeating block has retreated poleward to the Canadian Arctic while the West flattens and cools. That is a real shift from the "south-central ridge regenerates and endures" outcome the data pointed to a day ago.

Climate Context

The ONI table's "current" label lags its own data: the equatorial Pacific warmed out of last winter's weak La Niña, crossed positive in spring, and reaches roughly +1.80 by JJA 2026, so late September sits in a moderate-to-strong El Niño, not the labeled cool state. The tropics fit that state and the just-past hurricane-season peak with a textbook basin split — the Atlantic is quiet, with Tropical Storm Gonzalo near the Cabo Verde Islands and Tropical Depression Fay near the Azores both far offshore and no development expected in seven days, while the East Pacific is hyperactive with three simultaneous hurricanes (Polo near Cabo Corrientes, Odalys west-southwest of Baja, Nolo south of the Big Island), a Tehuantepec-area disturbance at 90 percent, and another likely depression east-southeast of Hawaii at 70 percent. The North American Monsoon is fading into its late-season phase but still supplies the Pacific-and-Gulf moisture overlap that, combined with Polo's tropical connection, will feed the Four Corners and Plains rains next week. Late-season severe weather remains in its expected ridge-periphery, southern-Plains mode rather than as deep-Plains outbreaks. Teleconnections are a weak warm-season cross-check but align with the height field: the sharply negative NAO supports the stalling Atlantic cutoff and the emerging high-latitude Arctic block, and the weakly negative PNA is consistent with the retreat of the western ridge.
