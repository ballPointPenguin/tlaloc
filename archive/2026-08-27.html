Synoptic Picture

Amplified western ridge and a central-U.S. cutoff trough steer the severe stream east while Southwest heat bakes on — and the ensembles say the ridge contracts and reloads south and east, not breaks down

The organizing story is a familiar late-summer dipole, now several days deep: a strongly amplified ridge over the Pacific Northwest and southwestern Canada (roughly 594 dam at the axis) stacked against a broad, blocked trough that runs from the central Rockies across the Plains into the Great Lakes, with what DuPage reads as a closed low over the Kansas-Oklahoma area (the 588 dam value on that closed contour should be read with caution). At the surface the same pattern shows up as a sprawling high-pressure complex — a stout 1032 mb center over the central Plains, a 1024 mb high over the interior West, and a 1034-1039 hPa ridge over the Canadian Prairies. The tightest gradient sits along the Gulf Coast, where that Plains high and a 1014 mb low over the western Gulf pinch the isobars into a focused southerly moisture corridor, feeding upper-70s dewpoints into the Mississippi and Tennessee valleys. A slow cold front arcs from a Canadian low through the Great Lakes and down through the Southeast, and it is the day's main lever for lift. Over Canada the interior stays settled under the broad high, with active weather confined to the Pacific side — a sub-1000 hPa low and front pressing into British Columbia and the Yukon — and to a loose, weak-gradient trough over the eastern provinces.

Today's centers of action ring the trough rather than sit under the ridge. The severe stream is northern-stream and frontal: SPC's Slight Risk covers the Mid-Atlantic/Northeast (two mesoscale discussions are running there this afternoon), the central Plains along the Nebraska-South Dakota border where deep-layer shear supports supercells and 2-inch hail, and central Montana behind a terrain-fired shortwave. WPC layers a heavy-rain and flash-flood threat along the slow front from the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic through the Southeast and Lower Mississippi Valley, with a separate arc of convection over the eastern Gulf Coast and Florida in the tropical airmass. The durable, dangerous element remains the heat: Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk with highs of 100-115 F over the Southwest and southern Texas and poor overnight recovery, a product of midlevel subsidence and warming, a strengthened cap, and dry-air entrainment under the ridge — not the modest surface highs beneath it. Monsoon moisture keeps daily high-terrain convection going across the Southwest and Rockies.

The upper structure ties it together. The Air Mass RGB shows a broad low-PV, ridge-subsidence expanse of red-orange over the Desert Southwest, southern Rockies, and northern Mexico — a warm dry airmass, not a stratospheric signature — bounded by a sharp airmass boundary from the central Plains southwestward into the Rockies that marks the implied jet corridor and the leading edge of the digging shortwave. A more compact, saturated dry streamer curling from the northern Rockies into the central Plains on the trough's cyclonic flank is a credible high-PV intrusion into the mid-troposphere. A 65-105 kt jet wraps cyclonically around the trough into the Great Lakes, consistent with the negatively tilted, northwest-to-southeast trough axis, while the eastern seaboard carries separate shortwave energy near 574-580 dam. Because the pattern is blocked rather than progressive, the central-U.S. trough is set to pivot and deamplify slowly in place rather than sweep cleanly offshore — which is why the front keeps recycling storms across the eastern half of the country. On the regime question, the ensemble pair points the same way as it has all week, toward contraction and reload rather than breakdown. The 6-10 day (Sep 1-5) throws the strongest positive height anomaly well north — over the Canadian Arctic and off the BC coast — while the western U.S. sits under a trough and the flow across the CONUS goes zonal to weakly troughed, with 90-100% above-normal temperature odds bullseyed over the central South. The 8-14 day (Sep 3-9) then flips that Arctic ridge into a deep trough over Baffin Island, leaves western Canada nearly zonal with no robust northern-Rockies ridge core, and relocates the 588 dam ridge core south to the Desert Southwest, southern Plains, and northern Mexico. WPC's extended discussion sharpens this: the upper high shifts eastward this weekend into next week, the Major-to-Extreme heat expands from the southern Plains northeastward into the Mid-South, Ohio Valley, and Southeast, and the Desert Southwest heat threat actually moderates. Read together, this is geographic contraction and an eastward/southward reload of the ridge with the West staying troughed and cool — not the collapse of a durable northern block, and not a wholesale regime change. It also refines yesterday's expectation: rather than the eastern cutoff cleanly departing northeast, the central trough has stayed amplified with a cutoff now over the southern Plains, though guidance still has it slowly deamplifying and the warmth marching east.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America running close to the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon is active and feeding daily high-terrain convection across the Southwest and Rockies, and severe weather is expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream and frontal storms rather than deep-Plains outbreaks. The ENSO backdrop is a decisively developing warm state — the ONI has climbed from weak La Niña in late 2025 through neutral in spring to roughly +1.4 C by late spring/early summer 2026, trending toward at least a moderate El Niño. That configuration typically favors an active eastern Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and even as the basin nears its early-September climatological peak the tropics fit that split: Tropical Storm Lala plus a strongly organized EP93 south of Baja (90%), EP94 east-southeast of Hawaii (70%), and additional candidate areas off Mexico and near Hawaii, against a single Atlantic wave that is near tropical-storm strength today (90%) but expected to run into hostile conditions and fade by Friday. Summer teleconnection indices are lagging, directionally suggestive cross-checks rather than drivers: a sharply negative NAO (-0.90 mean, -1.28 latest) and a slightly negative, weakening PNA (-0.17) are loosely consistent with sluggish Atlantic flow and a western ridge that is losing amplitude and retreating, while the AO's rebound and renewed slip underscore that the story is best read off the height and PV fields, not the indices.
