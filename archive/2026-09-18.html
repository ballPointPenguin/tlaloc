Synoptic Picture

A blocked western dipole and a deep Canadian trough hold the cool-north/hot-south split; ensembles keep retreating the heat ridge toward the Southwest and northern Mexico while the northern tier turns trough-dominant

The organizing story is a high-amplitude, blocked flow across the western two-thirds of the continent riding atop a persistent cool-north/hot-south dipole. At 500 mb (12Z) a deep, near-cutoff western trough sits over the Great Basin near Nevada-Utah (heights around 577 dam) with a slight negative tilt and closed contours — a diffluent, slow-mover that will pivot rather than race east — while a strongly amplified downstream ridge (594 dam or better) arcs from the central Plains north into the upper Midwest and Great Lakes. That amplification is a genuine step up from yesterday, when the western trough had opened and gone progressive; it has since re-amplified and closed off over the Great Basin, and the downstream ridge has consolidated over the central U.S. At the surface the ridge anchors a robust 1031 mb high over the upper Midwest, the strongest continental feature on the chart, while a cold front trails from a northern Rockies low complex southeast into the central Plains, merging near a 1013 mb low over Oklahoma with Gulf moisture (upper-60s to low-70s dewpoints) returning ahead of it. Canada is fully part of this: the CMC carries a broad 984 hPa low over/near Hudson Bay whose frontal system feeds the upper-Midwest low, a Pacific ridge (a 1039 hPa NE Pacific high) draped through British Columbia and Alberta, and a separate 983 hPa low off the Maritimes — a classic autumn-transitional setup with progressive mid-continent lows and a sharp Great Lakes-to-St. Lawrence baroclinic zone.

Today's focal points cluster on the ridge's northern and western flanks. The primary heavy-rain axis runs the northern tier — WPC flags repeated convection and a flash-flood threat across the northern Rockies, Great Lakes, Upper Mississippi Valley and Midwest through Saturday, shifting toward the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic late in the weekend — and the Air Mass RGB confirms it with a cluster of cold, deep-convective tops over the upper Midwest and Great Lakes tied to the surface cyclone and front. Residual monsoonal moisture keeps isolated storms over Southwest higher terrain, and organized convection is active over the western Gulf and Bay of Campeche within the deep tropical airmass covering the Gulf, Florida and Caribbean.

The upper-level structure ties it together and explains the persistence. The western cutoff paired with the amplified central ridge forms a blocked dipole that retards downstream translation — this is not a progressive regime. The broad orange-red drying across the interior West on the Air Mass RGB is warm-season, anticyclonic ridge-top dry air given its extent, not a stratospheric intrusion; the sharper, more compact dry streamer wrapping cyclonically from the Pacific Northwest into the northern Rockies marks the jet corridor on the trough's eastern edge, and that jet accelerates through the Plains and into the Great Lakes along the ridge's downstream flank — the forcing rail for the northern-tier rain. It is midlevel subsidence and warming, a strengthened cap and weak large-scale ascent beneath the southern ridge — not any single surface high — that keep the south-central heat locked in and its convection suppressed. Well downstream, a compact orange-red filament on the cyclonic side of the boundary over the Maritimes and northwest Atlantic is consistent with a stratospheric intrusion wrapping the offshore cutoff.

On the regime question, the two ensemble leads continue and sharpen yesterday's read. In the 6-10 day (Sep 23-27) the positive height anomaly is amplified but geographically confined offshore, over the northeastern Pacific and Gulf of Alaska into Alaska and the Yukon (+9 to +12 dam, axis near 130-135W), with the western CONUS only near-normal to slightly below, a downstream trough of -6 to -9 dam across the central and southern states, and a closed low over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. By the 8-14 day that offshore ridge flips to strongly negative — a trough reaching into western Canada — leaving no ridge over the northern Rockies or northern Plains, while the positive anomaly consolidates well to the south over the south-central U.S. and northern Mexico and the Archipelago low deepens. Read against each other, that pair says plainly the northern/western ridge does not regenerate in place: it retreats southward and the entire northern tier goes trough-dominant. This is ridge contraction and southwestward retreat, not a rebuild and not a clean collapse. WPC's extended discussion frames the same evolution — a Great Lakes upper low interacting with southern-stream Gulf energy over the East, a cold-air-damming wedge from a Quebec high cooling the Northeast/Mid-Atlantic, and an eastern Pacific/Southwest trough with the south-central ridge "gradually reducing in scope and magnitude" while NE Pacific troughing pushes toward the Northwest late next week. The honest uncertainty the guidance flags is how much southern-stream energy ejects northeast into the Plains versus ridging holding it off — model spread is real by days 6-7.
