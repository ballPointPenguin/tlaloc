Synoptic Picture

Southwest flood threat peaks as ejecting trough spawns Plains cyclone and two nights of severe storms

The western trough behind four days of Southwest flash flooding is finally lifting out, and as it goes it sets up a two-day run of severe weather and rapid cyclone development over the Plains. Today WPC has a Moderate excessive rainfall risk over southern Utah, northern Arizona and western Colorado. Storm totals of 2-4 inches on upslope terrain will fall on saturated soils as a low off Baja merges with the trough. Tonight SPC's Slight risk runs from New Mexico into the southern High Plains. A fast, organized line can bring gusts over 70 mph in east-central New Mexico and line-embedded tornadoes, which are rare overnight this late in the season. Thick cloud and a stable boundary layer are the limiting factors. On Monday a deep surface low develops over central Nebraska. SPC's Day 2 Slight risk covers Oklahoma, north Texas and south-central to eastern Kansas: supercells along the dryline after 21Z with a few tornadoes and a conditional EF2 risk, then a damaging-wind line overnight. In the East, a strong 1034 mb high keeps things dry and New England colder than normal, but its onshore flow is pushing heavy rain onto Florida's east coast.

At 500 mb the trough is open and positively tilted, with its axis near the California-Oregon coast and its base off Baja. On Air Mass RGB, dry upper-level air wraps around its western flank. A 60-65 kt jet streak over Southern California points southwest to northeast toward the northern Plains. Monday's stronger speed max crosses New Mexico into Kansas and Oklahoma and drives the Plains cyclone, while a 50-60 kt low-level jet carries mid-60s dewpoints north. Colder air behind the system brings heavy snow to the Central Rockies Tuesday under a Winter Storm Warning. Farther north, a 75-80+ kt northern-stream jet runs over Ontario and Quebec, and several lows are strung across Canada.

The cold front reaches the Mid/Lower Mississippi Valley Tuesday, then the Ohio/Tennessee Valleys and Southeast Wednesday, where WPC already has a Marginal rainfall risk. It reaches the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast Thursday and moves offshore late week, with cooler, drier air behind it. The main uncertainty is how the northern and southern streams phase, which controls how strong the low gets and where the heaviest rain falls. The western regime breaks rather than reloads. CPC's 6-10 and 8-14 day charts rebuild the trough over the Gulf of Alaska (about -15 to -21 dam) and put the ridge over the northern Plains and central Canada (+9 to +15 dam). That sends the Pacific storm track into British Columbia and the Northwest and favors above-normal temperatures across nearly the whole CONUS for November 6-10, with 70-90% odds along the northern border.

Climate Context

A strong El Niño frames this pattern. The Oceanic Niño Index reached +2.16°C for July-September, up from -0.39 last winter, and is still rising, though more slowly. A warm-season El Niño tends to strengthen the subtropical jet and the moisture supply into the Southwest, which fits this week's Baja-fed flooding. The Atlantic is quiet, with no tropical development expected over the next 7 days as hurricane season winds down. Early November is the Plains' secondary severe season, when strong jets can make up for marginal moisture, as they will tonight and Monday. The teleconnection indices are a month stale and are left out.
