Synoptic Picture

Blocked and welded: a Midwest cutoff and an offshore Northeast low flank an entrenched Southwest heat ridge, with the north-central Plains carrying the severe stream

The organizing story is a blocked, high-amplitude late-summer pattern in which a genuine cutoff low anchors the continental interior while durable heat stays welded to the Southwest. The 12Z 500 mb analysis puts a closed low over the upper Midwest near the Iowa-Wisconsin border, heights around 570 dam with contours wrapping fully around the core — no longer the near-cutoff of yesterday but a discrete, self-retaining circulation cut off from the westerlies. To its west a broad ridge runs from the Desert Southwest through the central Rockies (592-594 dam over the Four Corners), and a second, colder closed or nearly closed low sits off the Mid-Atlantic coast. That is the geometry to name carefully: a quasi-stationary western ridge flanked by two distinct cyclonic centers to its north and east, not a Rex block, which would require an anticyclone stacked poleward of a cutoff at the same longitude. This is the third day of the same amplified, poorly progressive regime, but the flanks keep reorganizing: the interior trough Tlaloc tracked yesterday has fully closed off, and Canada has flipped from headline-maker to bystander — the deep 990 mb Prairie low is gone, replaced by a broad 1030 mb high ridging across the southern Prairies and settling the interior.

The centers of action ring the interior cutoff and hug the ridge. The dangerous, durable element is heat: WPC carries Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk across the Southern Plains, Four Corners, and Lower Mississippi Valley, with highs in the upper 90s to 100s, heat indices of 105-115, numerous daily records, and little overnight relief. Beneath that ridge the suppression and baking come from midlevel subsidence and warming, a strengthened cap, dry-air entrainment, and weak large-scale ascent — the modest 1021-1022 mb surface highs on the map are late-summer values that carry no dynamical weight of their own. On the ridge's cool, disturbed northwestern flank, a shortwave crossing the Pacific Northwest into the northern Rockies drives today's severe threat: SPC's Slight Risk over the northern Rockies/Plains and northern Great Basin features 30-50 kt deep-layer shear on weak instability, large hail and damaging wind, and isolated gusts potentially topping 75 mph in Montana from upscale multicell/supercell growth. That northern-stream threat persists into Monday — SPC's Day 2 Marginal keeps a hail/wind and 75+ mph gust potential across the northern Plains behind a Dakotas cold front, and adds a transient-supercell threat over Maine as the Mid-Atlantic upper low ejects northeast beneath a 60 kt jet. Repeated convection north of the ridge keeps a flash-flood concern going, and North American Monsoon moisture sustains daily high-terrain storms across the Southwest and Intermountain West.

The upper structure ties it together and sets up the key regime question. The implied jet wraps cyclonically around the Midwest cutoff, and the Air Mass RGB shows the payoff — a broad rust-orange plume of dry, subsident upper-tropospheric air (ridge air, not a stratospheric intrusion) over the central and southeastern U.S., bounded by a sharp airmass boundary from the upper Midwest south through the central Plains that marks the implied jet corridor between that ridge air to the east and the cooler, disturbed regime to the west, with cold-topped convection firing on that boundary near the Nebraska-South Dakota border. On whether the block breaks or merely contracts, the ensemble means argue contraction, continuing yesterday's read. The 6-10 day (Aug 28-Sep 1) concentrates the positive height anomaly (near +15 dam) over the northeastern Pacific and the BC/Yukon coast with the zero line cutting through the interior West, so the ridge does not reach the central Plains and flow over the central and eastern U.S. trends more progressive. The 8-14 day (Aug 30-Sep 5) then removes any ridge axis over western Canada and the northern Rockies entirely, pushes the 588 dam contour south into the Southwest and northern Mexico, and deepens the Arctic trough with below-normal heights spilling into the northern and central Plains. Read as a pair, the northern half of the western ridge gives way while the core retreats toward the Southwest and northern Mexico — a geographic contraction with the north cooling and the heat staying pinned to the south, not a wholesale collapse to zonal flow.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America running close to the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon is active and feeding high-terrain Southwest convection, while severe weather is expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream storms along the cool flank of the heat ridge rather than classic deep-Plains supercells. The ENSO backdrop is a decisively developing warm state — the ONI has climbed from a weak cool episode in late 2025 through neutral in spring to roughly +1.39 by May-June-July 2026, a clear trend toward at least a moderate El Nino. That configuration typically favors the eastern Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and today's tropics fit the split as the basin approaches its early-September climatological peak: the East Pacific is busy (Tropical Storms Lala and Moke, a gale-force low southwest of Baja near 100 percent formation odds, plus two more areas at 40-60 percent), while the Atlantic shows only low-end features — a subtropical chance west of Portugal, a 30 percent area southeast of Bermuda, and a 50 percent wave expected off West Africa. Summer teleconnection indices are a weak, lagging cross-check on the height field and carry modest magnitudes: a decisively negative NAO (-0.90 mean, -1.28 latest) is loosely consistent with retaining the cutoffs, while a slightly negative, falling PNA and a positive AO (unfavorable for high-latitude blocking) offer only directional support to the amplified interior pattern the height and PV fields already show.
