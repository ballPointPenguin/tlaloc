Synoptic Picture

Today's map organizes around a decidedly blocked, high-amplitude ridge-trough-ridge longwave pattern. This is a true cutoff isolated from the westerlies — it will pivot slowly rather than translate — so the southern stream is blocked while the northern stream stays progressive. The result is a clean three-way division of labor: heat in the West, flooding in the South, and progressive fronts across the North. The focal points fall straight out of the split. First, heat: WPC flags an anomalous October heatwave across California and the Great Basin at 15-20 degrees above normal, highs in the 90s to near 100 in the valleys with little overnight relief and the heat expanding across the Great Basin. The mechanism is the building upper ridge — subsidence, midlevel warming, and dry-air entrainment under the broad orange-red western dry signature — not the 1026 mb surface high over the Colorado-Utah border, which is a bystander. Second, flooding: the slow Southwest cutoff and the surface low near the Texas-Mexico border anchor an excessive-rainfall axis along the Gulf Coast and Southeast through Sunday, with up to 7 inches possible near the Alabama coast and the heavy-rain threat shifting northeast into the Carolinas by Sunday; deep convection is already firing over the Texas-Louisiana Gulf Coast and in a second cluster over the eastern Gulf south of the Florida Panhandle. The blocked upper pattern is why all of this persists: the implied jet runs around the east flank of the cutoff and northeastward into the Ohio Valley at 60-80 kt, with the sharpest airmass boundary — dry orange giving way to moist green from eastern Texas toward the Tennessee Valley — marking the frontal/jet corridor that keeps the Gulf rain lit. The week's real question is regime change versus temporary flattening, and the ensemble pair answers it cleanly. The 6-10 day (Oct 8-12) anchors the positive height anomaly offshore over the Gulf of Alaska and British Columbia (+6 to +8 dam) with the ridge held near the coast — the zero line pulled back to the Rockies, so the ridge never penetrates the interior — paired with a Hudson Bay trough; at the surface this still supports a high-confidence warm interior West (90-100% above-normal over Nevada and Utah). That continues and firms up yesterday's read. The modestly negative PNA is a consistent cross-check on the absence of a durable inland western ridge; treat it and the indices as confirmation of a story the height and PV fields already tell.
