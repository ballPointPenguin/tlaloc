Synoptic Picture

The summer block rearranges again: the heat ridge re-expands west over the interior while a negatively tilted trough digs into the north-central CONUS and lights up the ridge's northern rim

The peak-summer block that Tlaloc has tracked for roughly two weeks holds, but its geometry has flipped once more. Yesterday the heat ridge sat over the central and northern Plains with a Pacific Northwest trough digging in behind it; today the ridge has done what WPC's guidance advertised and re-expanded westward, consolidating as a high-amplitude 500 mb dome over the interior West with heights near 5960 dm over the Desert Southwest and reaching north into the Pacific Northwest. In its place, a deep, slightly negatively tilted longwave trough has dug into the north-central CONUS, its axis running from the central Plains into the Mississippi Valley, with a blocked, nearly stagnant cutoff feature marked at 500 mb over central Kansas. That ridge-west/trough-central arrangement is the single story on the map: it bakes the interior beneath the ridge, drives the polar jet and its shortwaves through the northern tier, and forces the day's convective action onto the trough's northern and eastern rim rather than through its stalled core.

The centers of action follow directly. The primary severe focus today is the Upper Midwest and Great Lakes, where SPC carries an Enhanced Risk: strong northwesterly mid-level flow overrunning very warm, moist, unstable air supports intense supercells from northern Minnesota into Wisconsin, with large to very large hail, damaging winds potentially topping 75 mph, and a tornado risk, before upscale growth into wind-producing clusters. A secondary damaging-wind threat runs through the Middle Mississippi Valley, and a corridor across Nebraska, eastern South Dakota, and western Iowa near the surface trough can see 60-80 mph gusts. That engine propagates east tomorrow: SPC's Day 2 outlook hangs a Slight Risk from the Great Lakes into the Ohio Valley with MLCAPE of 2500-3500+ J/kg and a corridor from lower Michigan into Indiana, Ohio, and Kentucky flagged for a possible Enhanced upgrade as storms coalesce into MCSs Monday night. Overlaying everything is dangerous heat: WPC advertises highs of 95-105F across the central and southern U.S. and 115-120F in the Southwest, with widespread Major to Extreme HeatRisk. Canada carries real weight today, too. A dominant 1036 hPa high off the BC coast is the northern extension of the western ridge, keeping the interior hot and dry, while a sprawling low complex covers central and eastern Canada and a deep 979 hPa low near the Maritimes tightens the gradient over the Gulf of St. Lawrence, driving the most organized Canadian precipitation along that corridor.

Aloft, the block explains both extremes. Air Mass RGB shows a vivid orange-red stratospheric dry intrusion with high potential vorticity streaming from the northern Rockies through Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas, with a sharp PV gradient marking the jet axis from the Tennessee Valley into the central Appalachians; deep tropical moisture (green) floods the Gulf Coast and Florida beneath the jet exit region. Two jet streaks stand out near 60-70 kt, one over the Pacific Northwest into northern Montana and a second over the Ohio Valley into the Mid-Atlantic, the latter shearing the convective band and offshore frontal wave the satellite flags off the Carolinas and Mid-Atlantic coast. This is the classic back-of-the-trough, northwest-flow severe regime, and with the trough amplified and slow to progress, storm motions stay slow enough to keep a heavy-rain and flash-flood signal riding along with the wind and hail threat. The full-disk imagery adds a high-latitude note: a strong stratospheric intrusion digging from Hudson Bay northwestward marks a deep Arctic trough, the far end of the same amplified wave train.

Climate Context

This is textbook late-July North America: the North American Monsoon is in full swing over the Desert Southwest and southern Rockies, and mid-summer heat-and-severe season is driving both the entrenched interior heat dome and the marginal severe and flash-flood threats along the trough's periphery. The CPC 6-10 day outlook reinforces the persistence, with a 90-100 percent above-normal temperature bull's-eye over the Four Corners and no meaningful trough intrusion expected to break the western ridge before mid-August. The hurricane basins remain split: the Atlantic, Caribbean, and Gulf are quiet with no development expected over the next seven days despite the climatological ramp-up, while the East Pacific stays busy, with Hurricane Fausto crossing into the Central Pacific, Hurricane Genevieve southwest of Mexico, and a broad low southwest of Hawaii given a 90 percent chance of developing as it nears the Date Line. The ENSO backdrop is one of steady cold-to-warm transition: after last year's weak La Nina near -0.5 C, the ONI has warmed through neutral toward warm-phase territory, approaching +1.0 C by the spring quarter, a shift that will increasingly shape the back half of the Atlantic season.
