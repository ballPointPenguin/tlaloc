Synoptic Picture

The late-summer dipole flattens toward progressive flow — but the Southwest heat ridge contracts and reloads eastward rather than breaking down, keeping dangerous heat over the southern tier while a Plains cold front carries the severe stream

The amplified western-ridge/eastern-trough dipole that has organized North America for a week is losing amplitude on its eastern half. The discrete New England cutoff Tlaloc tracked yesterday has washed out: today's 12Z 500 mb analysis shows no closed contour anywhere in the domain, just a broad, weakly amplified, near-neutrally tilted trough from the Upper Midwest into the lower Mississippi Valley with a more distinct shortwave (heights near 570-577 dam, -10 to -14 C) over the Ohio Valley and Great Lakes. The western heat ridge, by contrast, holds firm over the Desert Southwest and southern Rockies, with the highest heights near 592-594 dam over Arizona and New Mexico. The overall flow is now progressive rather than blocked. At the surface the organizing feature is a 1006 mb low over Minnesota-Wisconsin whose cold front trails southwest into the central Plains with a warm front toward the Great Lakes — the tightest gradient on the map runs along and behind that front across the Midwest. Canada re-enters the story on both flanks: a formidable 1037 hPa high dominates the Hudson Bay/eastern interior, while a deep 986 hPa Pacific low drives a cold front toward the BC coast, tightening the gradient across the Coast Mountains.

Today's centers of action divide neatly between the ridge and its periphery. The durable, dangerous element remains the heat: WPC carries record-breaking highs of 100-110 F across the southern Plains and up to 115 F in the Desert Southwest with Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk and record-warm overnight lows limiting recovery — suppression and baking that come from midlevel subsidence and warming, a strengthened cap, dry-air entrainment, and weak large-scale ascent, not from the modest 1020 mb summer high beneath it. The severe stream rings the ridge: SPC's Day 1 Slight Risk covers the central and southern Plains, where afternoon supercells with large hail across western/southern Nebraska into Kansas are expected to grow upscale into damaging-wind clusters (75+ mph possible) sweeping into the Panhandles, plus a separate Slight over eastern Minnesota and northwest Wisconsin on the advancing front. The Southeast sees repeated pulse storms with a damaging-wind threat along a stalled front, and monsoon moisture keeps daily high-terrain convection and flash-flood risk going across the Southwest and Rockies. In the tropics the ENSO-favored basin is doing the work — three named East Pacific storms plus a tropical wave at 90% — while the Atlantic offers only low-end features; a compact, partially organized convective cluster near the Cuban longitude south of Florida is worth watching.

The upper structure ties it together and frames the week's key question. The airmass RGB shows a sharp boundary from the central Plains northeast to the Great Lakes marking the implied jet corridor that steers the severe convection, with a compact, curving orange-red streamer arching from the southern Rockies across northern Texas into the lower Mississippi Valley — a plausible stratospheric-intrusion/high-PV signature on the trough's eastern flank, distinct from the broad ridge-top subsidence over the Desert Southwest. On the regime question, the ensemble pair is the test. The 6-10 day (Aug 30-Sep 3) keeps the strong positive anomaly (~+90 m) confined offshore over the northeastern Pacific, with the zero line along the Pacific coast, a deep trough from the Canadian Arctic through central Canada to the Great Lakes, and near-to-below-normal heights across most of the CONUS. The 8-14 day (Sep 1-7) deepens the Arctic closed low and, critically, shows no positive anomaly over western Canada or the northern Rockies: the ridge core has retreated toward the southwestern U.S. and northern Mexico (588 dam contour there), while a positive height signal builds into the south-central and southeastern U.S. Read as a pair, this is geographic contraction, not collapse — the interior and northern amplification break down toward progressive flow under a deepening Canadian trough, but the ridge does not vanish; it shrinks toward the Southwest/northern Mexico and reloads warmth into the south. That is a genuine revision of yesterday's tentative read, which hinted at a negative anomaly migrating onto Texas and a real south-central relaxation by early September. WPC's day 3-7 discussion settles the ambiguity in the same direction: the ridge flattens on its northern side late this week but the closed high repositions eastward this weekend, refocusing and expanding hazardous heat across the southern Plains, south-central U.S., and Mid-South. The honest read: the heat regime is relocating and reloading eastward, not ending, while the northern tier genuinely cools.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America close to the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon is active and feeding daily high-terrain Southwest convection, and severe weather is expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream storms and frontal supercells rather than classic deep-Plains outbreaks. The ENSO backdrop is a decisively developing warm state — the ONI has climbed from weak La Niña in late 2025 through neutral in spring to roughly +1.39 C by May-June-July 2026, a clear trend toward at least a moderate El Niño. That configuration typically favors an active eastern Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and even as the basin nears its early-September climatological peak the tropics fit that split: three named East Pacific storms plus a high-probability wave against only low-end Atlantic prospects. Summer teleconnection indices are weak, lagging cross-checks on the height field, not drivers of it: a decisively negative NAO (-1.28) and a weakly negative PNA are loosely consistent with the progressive, non-amplified continental pattern and the western ridge's failure to build northward, but they should be read as no more than a nod to the story the height and PV fields already tell.
