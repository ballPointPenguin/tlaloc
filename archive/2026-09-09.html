Synoptic Picture

A deep northern-Plains cutoff finally drives its front through, breaking the south-central heat — while the ridge retreats to the southern tier for good

The organizing feature over North America today is the northern cyclone Tlaloc has tracked all week, now matured into its most vigorous state. The surface low that was 999-1000 mb over the upper Great Lakes yesterday has deepened to roughly 988 mb near the US-Canada border in the northern Plains, the strongest cyclone on the WPC chart, and aloft it has closed off into a deep 500 mb low near 564 dam over the northern Plains and southern Prairies. That is a genuine cutoff — the tight contour packing and light core winds confirm it — a marked step up from the shallow, high-heights perturbation that carried the "cutoff" caveat a day or two ago. Its trailing cold front, holding the tightest surface gradient on the map from the central Plains into Texas, is the front that breaks the entrenched south-central heat by Thursday. East of the trough the CONUS sits under broadly anticyclonic, near-zonal flow with heights of 588-594 dam, so the story is a sharp west-versus-east split: a blocked, pivoting cutoff in the north-central interior against progressive ridging downstream.

Today's centers of action string along the front and its periphery. SPC carries a Slight Risk from central Ohio to western New York for organized line segments and damaging wind, with a low-end tornado risk in western Pennsylvania and New York where low-level curvature and a strengthening jet arrive after 00Z; storms in the central Plains-to-Ohio Valley corridor are more outflow-dominant and disorganized. Heavy rain is the co-threat, focusing over the central Appalachians on Thursday as frontal waves slow, and lingering showers persist along a dissipating boundary across the Southeast. The other rain focus is the North American Monsoon, with a Slight Risk for excessive rainfall in southeastern Arizona into southwestern New Mexico today. On the surface moisture side, deep Gulf air with upper-70s dewpoints is pooling ahead of the front over Louisiana and Mississippi, the region's best convergence-plus-high-PW combination. In Canada, the active weather is offshore: a sprawling North Pacific low complex near 987 hPa with a strong 1035 hPa high to its southwest drives a tight gradient and an active frontal fetch onto the BC and Alaska coasts, while the interior is slack.

The Air Mass RGB ties it together: the sharp orange-to-green boundary from the central Great Lakes southwest into the central Plains marks the jet corridor doing the dynamical work on the cyclone, and the compact cyclonically curved red streamer curling into the Ohio Valley and New England is a dry intrusion on the trough's forward flank. The broad orange-red over the central and southern Plains is warm-season low-PV subsidence air under the late-summer ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion — and midlevel warming and capping under it, not any single surface high, are why the heat has held until the front's arrival. The geometry is worth naming precisely: the anticyclone anchoring the west sits over the Pacific Northwest and western Canada, well northwest of rather than directly poleward of the cutoff, so this is an amplified ridge-cutoff dipole, not a Rex block. On evolution, the ensemble pair confirms the transition Tlaloc flagged yesterday. At 6-10 days the western ridge has already retreated northward — western-CONUS anomalies near zero, flow flattening — with the positive anomaly displaced into the Canadian Arctic; by 8-14 days that Arctic ridge has collapsed into a closed Arctic low, western Canada and the northern Rockies stay trough-dominant, and the surviving ridge consolidates across the southern tier with the 588 dam contour arched poleward from the Desert Southwest to the Gulf. This is the classic geographic contraction — the ridge retreating toward the Southwest and southern Plains as a heat anchor while the north and west go mobile and cool — not a poleward regeneration and not a full breakdown. The WPC extended discussion supports it: a shortwave and cold front sweep the northern tier this weekend, a compact closed low drops into the Northwest and progresses to the northern Plains by Monday with moderate-to-heavy rain and northern Rockies wintry potential, another deeper wave follows Tuesday-Wednesday, and southern-tier ridging persists to keep the Southern Plains heat and Four Corners monsoon going.

The tropics are behaving to type: a completely suppressed Atlantic, where NHC expects no development for seven days at what is climatologically the season's peak, set against a busy East Pacific — Tropical Storm Lowell north-northwest of Hawaii, a well-organized low south-southwest of southern Baja with an 80% chance of becoming a depression within 48 hours, and a second low with modest odds behind it. That basin split is a textbook El Niño signature. Seasonally, the North American Monsoon remains active across the Southwest and interior Mexico, and late-summer severe weather is running in its expected ridge-periphery and frontal mode along the northern tier rather than as deep-Plains outbreaks. The most durable regional signal stays the south-central heat, which WPC expects to linger over the Southern Plains with Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk and record-tying temperatures into next week even as the front knocks it back farther north.
