Synoptic Picture

The south-central heat ridge digs in and contracts toward the southern tier while an eastern-Pacific trough finally cools the West — the storm track stays pinned to the ridge's northern flank, with an Ohio Valley flash-flood bull's-eye

The amplified summer pattern Tlaloc has tracked for a week is now entering its next, cleaner phase. At 500 mb a broad high-amplitude ridge still crowns the Upper Midwest and Great Lakes (core near 588-594 dam), but the durable heat has consolidated to its south, from the Central Plains through the Tennessee Valley and Southeast, and the western side of the pattern is finally moving. Yesterday's near-cutoff low sitting offshore of the Pacific Northwest has come ashore as an open longwave trough over western Canada and the Northwest, and WPC has temperatures falling 10-20 degrees west of the Continental Divide by Thursday. This is the geographic contraction the ensembles have been advertising: the ridge is not breaking down so much as retreating toward the Southwest and northern Mexico while the West cools underneath the incoming trough. (Note the Canadian surface analysis in today's data is mislabeled to March and reads more winter-like than August; the western cooldown is better carried by the 500 mb field and WPC than by that chart.)

The centers of action all ring the ridge and its northern jet. The most consequential is hydrologic: WPC flags impactful flash flooding in the Ohio Valley today, 2-4 inches onto ground already saturated by earlier rounds — the continuation of the multi-day heavy-rain axis draped along the slow, wavy frontal zone. On the severe side, SPC carries an Enhanced risk from northern Illinois into the Ohio Valley, where a morning MCS out of eastern Iowa is expected to organize into wind-producing linear clusters (gusts to 60-85 mph) as it rides the CAPE gradient, with a broader Slight risk from Iowa to the Carolinas. Well up the ridge's northwest flank, the northern High Plains see scattered supercells with large-to-very-large hail near a stationary front. Beneath the ridge core, the south-central to Southeast heat dome brings upper 90s to triple digits and record-challenging warmth, with convection suppressed by midlevel subsidence, a strong cap and dry air aloft rather than by the modest surface highs. Southwest monsoon convection rounds out the map.

The upper-level structure ties it together. A west-to-east airmass boundary across the central Plains into the Mid-Atlantic on the Air Mass RGB marks the implied jet corridor, with a 65-75 kt streak arcing around the ridge's northern flank; the broad red-orange plume to the south is warm, dry ridge-top air, not a stratospheric intrusion, while a compact reddish streamer wrapping the trough near the central Appalachians is a genuine dry intrusion on that feature's western side. This arrangement is self-reinforcing in the near term: WPC's extended discussion carries high confidence through next week that the southern ridge persists while progressive northern-stream shortwaves keep crossing the wavy front, so the repeat-convection heavy-rain threat and the heat both hold. The genuine regime question, as it has been all week, sits at high latitudes and the ensembles still cannot settle it. On the CONUS the two leads agree — the 6-10 day (Aug 16-20) and 8-14 day (Aug 18-24) both keep the 588-594 dam ridge over the southern tier, with the 8-14 explicitly retreating its core toward northern Mexico and the Southwest and digging a trough down the interior West, matching the 6-10 day's cool lean over Nevada and the Great Basin. But over the Canadian Arctic the charts openly contradict each other, one reading the Baffin Island feature as an anomalously high blocking ridge and the other as a deep cold cutoff at essentially the same absolute height — so the Arctic outcome is unresolved. The strongly negative NAO (-1.28, 7-day mean -0.90) is a lagged nudge toward high-latitude blocking, but with summer teleconnection weakness the height field has to carry the story, and here it does not yet.

Regional Signals

Two SPC mesoscale discussions are active, both at 40 percent watch probability: MCD 1919 over northern and central Iowa, where a strengthening low/mid-level circulation with rear-inflow could organize the morning storms into a more coherent, gusty cluster (55-70 mph, hail to 1.75 inch) through early afternoon, and MCD 1920 over east-central Indiana into southwestern Ohio, tracking a linear band capable of locally damaging gusts of 55-70 mph. Beyond the Ohio Valley flash-flood focus, WPC's extended outlook introduces Excessive Rainfall risk over the south-central Rockies for Friday, where slow-moving monsoon storms threaten flash flooding in burn scars, slot canyons and urban areas — the same downdraft-driven, isolated-severe threat SPC notes today over the Mojave.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America running near the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon is active over the Southwest, Great Basin and northern Mexico, and severe-weather season is expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream convection along a wavy front rather than classic Plains supercell setups. The ENSO backdrop is a developing/weak El Niño — the ONI trend climbs out of last year's weak La Niña through neutral late winter into warm territory peaking near +1.4 C by June-July 2026. That state typically favors the East Pacific while shear-suppresses the Atlantic, and today's tropics fit the split: the East Pacific carries a 60 percent case east-southeast of Hawaii (worth Hawaiian attention) and a 40 percent system off Baja, against a still-modest Atlantic. But the Atlantic is not dormant — the Cabo Verde low is up to 70 percent over seven days as the basin ramps toward its early-September climatological peak, so the current relative lull warrants monitoring rather than complacency.
