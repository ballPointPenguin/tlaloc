Synoptic Picture

A west-ridge/east-cutoff split organizes the map: entrenched Southwest heat, a northern-Plains wave cyclone driving severe, and a slow eastern cutoff lingering near the Great Lakes

The multi-week amplified regime, now in roughly its fifth day, has simplified from yesterday's near-omega look into a cleaner west-ridge/east-trough dipole. A robust western ridge holds firm at 592-594 dam over the Great Basin and fans from the Desert Southwest through the Intermountain West, while the trough that dug all the way to the Gulf 24 hours ago has lifted northeast and consolidated into a slow, near-cutoff 500 mb low around 572 dam near the Great Lakes and southeastern Canada. The 500 mb field reads quasi-blocked and non-progressive: the western ridge resists eastward progression and the detached eastern low will pivot slowly rather than translate. At the surface the primary engine is a 998 mb wave cyclone on the Montana-Saskatchewan border, with a cold front trailing southeast toward the central Gulf Coast and a warm front arcing to the upper Great Lakes. Canada carries real weight today as well: a deep 986 hPa low straddles the Alberta-British Columbia border, and a 1036 hPa northeastern Pacific high compresses the isobars along the BC coast into brisk onshore flow. East of the Rockies the surface gradient is genuinely slack — the scattered 1010-1018 mb highs over the interior are weak features and are not doing the heavy lifting in the story.

The centers of action ring the pattern. The northern-stream wave cyclone anchors today's severe threat: SPC carries a Slight Risk across the Upper Midwest, with morning storms over the Red River Valley reinforcing the frontal zone and afternoon heating supporting damaging gusts, large hail, and isolated tornadoes; WPC extends this Monday-Tuesday and shifts a heavy-rain corridor into the Upper Great Lakes, Mississippi Valley, and Central Plains by Wednesday. The eastern cutoff's front is the second focus — the bright, cold-topped convective band along the East Coast on the Air Mass imagery corresponds to WPC's Slight Risk excessive-rainfall areas over the northern Mid-Atlantic, New England, the Carolina coast, and eastern-coast Florida, easing to Marginal midweek as the boundary stalls. The North American Monsoon is the third: SPC holds a Slight Risk for 60-75 mph downburst winds across southeast Arizona and WPC a flash-flood threat across the Southwest. Along the southern tier, a stationary front from the Texas-Mexico border to the Southeast sits in rich Gulf moisture (dewpoints near 80 F), and ECCC flags the remnants of post-tropical Genevieve as a 1000 mb low over the central/southern U.S. feeding that moisture, though its placement is uncertain and NHC now shows no active system in either basin.

Aloft the surface story hangs together. The sharp Air Mass RGB boundary running northwest-to-southeast from the upper Midwest to the Mid-Atlantic marks the baroclinic zone and implied jet corridor on the cutoff's southwestern flank, which is where both the eastern front and the East Coast convection are anchored. The broad red-orange over the central and eastern U.S. is warm, dry, subsident mid- and upper-tropospheric air under the ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion. The teleconnection suite is directionally consistent — PNA strongly positive (+0.74) for western ridging over eastern troughing, AO sharply positive (+0.95) for weak high-latitude blocking, and NAO positive (+0.71) favoring easier ejection of eastern cutoffs — but with summer's muted signal these are cross-checks on a story the height and PV fields already tell, not the basis for it.

On the regime question, the ensemble pair points to a genuine transition rather than a cosmetic flattening, though confidence drops with lead time. The 6-10 day (valid Aug 8-12) still shows a strong positive height anomaly of +40 to +50 m over the south-central U.S. and central Plains and wall-to-wall above-normal temperatures, but its western footprint is already contracting eastward, with the zero-anomaly line back near 110W. WPC's days 3-7 discussion bridges this: the Southwest ridge intensifies and expands north and east into late week, building heat toward the Plains, but a shortwave dropping into the Northwest "may finally act to erode the ridge," and the Southwest ridge separates from the subtropical Atlantic ridge, opening a lane for tropical moisture into the Southeast. By the 8-14 day (valid Aug 10-16) the central-U.S. ridge does not hold: the positive core relocates to the high Canadian Arctic (+6 to +9 dam), the central and eastern U.S. shift to a negative anomaly with a trough from the Great Lakes to the Gulf, the West develops a modest north-south trough along the Rockies, and only the Southwest and northern Mexico remain under the ridge's flank. So the amplification is shrinking over the CONUS — part classic southwestward retreat toward the ridge's climatological home, part northward relocation of the core to an unusual high-latitude Arctic ridge — leaving the northern Plains and interior increasingly under trough influence late in the period.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America, and the pattern is textbook for the season: the heat-and-severe regime drives both entrenched warmth under the western/Southwest ridge — WPC continues Key Messages for a long-lasting, dangerous heat wave with widespread Moderate and isolated Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk across the Southwest and Intermountain West, building northeastward toward the High Plains and Mid-South — and the marginal severe and heavy-rain threats ringing the ridge along the frontal boundaries. The North American Monsoon is in full swing over Arizona and New Mexico, feeding the Southwest flood and downburst threat. The ENSO backdrop is one of steady cold-to-warm transition: the ONI table runs from a weak La Niña in late 2025 (around -0.5) through neutral to a warm peak near +0.98 in April-June 2026, placing early August under warm-ENSO/weak El Niño influence, though the provided table does not extend past spring. Both hurricane basins remain quiet against the climatological ramp toward the September peak, with NHC expecting no development in the Atlantic or East Pacific over the next seven days; former East Pacific storm Genevieve now survives only as inland post-tropical remnants.
