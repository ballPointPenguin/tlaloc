Synoptic Picture

The long-advertised ridge retreat arrives: the northern tier goes progressive as the heat consolidates over the lower Mississippi Valley

The blocked western dipole that organized the past several days is coming apart on schedule. The amplified ridge that stood over the central Plains and Great Lakes as recently as yesterday has gone progressive; in its place a northern-stream trough is sweeping from the northern Plains through the Great Lakes toward the Northeast, and the surviving ridge has consolidated well to the south — the 594 dam subtropical ridge axis now sits over Louisiana rather than the upper Midwest. The western cutoff endures but has drifted north to the Montana-Wyoming border (near 579 dam), still a self-retaining, pivoting feature that keeps flow blocked across the Rockies. At the surface a sprawling high dominates the central and eastern US (1028 mb over the upper Midwest, 1034 mb over the mid-Atlantic), leaving a slack interior gradient, while the day's real forcing rides the trailing cold front from the California-Nevada low complex southeast through the southern Plains. The cool-north/hot-south split that has defined the week persists, but its pivot has slid south.

Today's centers of action are the northern tier and the front. WPC's primary hazard is heavy rain and flash flooding across the Midwest, Ohio Valley and Great Lakes today over already-saturated soils, expanding into the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast Sunday; the Air Mass RGB confirms it with the parent cyclone's cold cloud shield arcing across the Great Lakes into New England. Severe weather is a secondary, Slight-risk story along the southbound cold front through the central and southern Plains — 60-75 mph wind gusts and large hail, a few supercells possible near Kansas, and a High Plains cluster organizing from Colorado into Kansas. On the ridge's southern flank the heat holds across the south-central US, lower Mississippi Valley and Southeast, with highs in the 90s to low 100s. In the deep tropical airmass over the Gulf and Caribbean, a 500 mb low southeast of Florida and convective clusters over the western Caribbean, Yucatan and southeastern Gulf keep south Florida wet.

The upper structure ties it together. The Montana-Wyoming closed low blocks the West while the tightly packed jet corridor along the northern tier — implied speeds above 70 kt northeast of the Great Lakes — is the forcing rail carrying the rain shield east. The broad orange-red drying across the interior West on the Air Mass RGB is warm-season, anticyclonic ridge-top dry air given its extent, not a stratospheric intrusion; the sharper, compact cyclonic streamer curling from the northern Rockies into the Plains marks the jet and its dry intrusion on the trough's eastern flank. Well downstream, a genuine stratospheric intrusion — a compact high-PV filament wrapping the offshore cyclone — shows up on the full-disk imagery over the northwestern Atlantic. The heat's persistence in the south is midlevel subsidence and warming and a strengthened cap beneath the ridge, not the sprawling surface high, which is too weak-gradient to carry that explanation.

Read against each other, the two ensemble leads say the retreat is real rather than cosmetic. The 6-10 day (Sep 24-28) shows a strongly amplified but poleward-displaced ridge over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, with the West Coast only near the zero-anomaly line — no ridge over the Rockies or British Columbia — and a downstream trough of modest negative anomalies over the central and eastern US. By the 8-14 day (Sep 26-Oct 2) that Arctic ridge flips outright to a deep cold-core low over the Archipelago, and the surviving ridge is confined to the eastern Pacific across the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico, leaving the northern tier trough-dominant. That pair settles the regime question: the heat ridge does not rebuild in the north; it contracts and retreats southwestward while the north cools. WPC's extended discussion frames the same evolution and offers the flattening-versus-regime-change test directly — a shortwave nudging into the Dakotas Thursday only temporarily disrupts the surviving southern ridge before feeding the eastern trough, and the south-central heat gradually reduces in scope and magnitude. The honest caveats: coastal-low placement off the East Coast next week and how the Pacific stream separates late in the period both carry real model spread.
