Synoptic Picture

A blocked dipole sharpens: a Great Basin cutoff faces an eastern ridge, dangerous heat consolidates from the south-central U.S. to the Southeast, and the ensembles retreat the ridge core toward the Desert Southwest

The organizing story remains the amplified, blocked dipole that has dominated Tlaloc's discussions all week, but its western pole has sharpened. The flat, diffuse western trough of yesterday has wound up into a genuine cutoff low near the Nevada-Utah-Idaho border, reading roughly 597 dam at its core and isolated from the westerlies by the surrounding ridge heights; its neutral-to-slightly-negative tilt and cutoff status mean it will pivot in place rather than sprint downstream. Opposite it, an eastern ridge of 588-592 dam sits over the Mid-Atlantic and Southeast. This is a ridge-cutoff dipole displaced across longitudes, not a Rex block — the eastern anticyclonic heights are well east of the western cutoff, not stacked poleward of it — but the effect is the same: the westerlies are pinched and neither feature translates quickly. At the surface the action rings the block. A 1008 mb low over the northern Plains/upper Midwest trails a cold front southwestward with a warm front into the Great Lakes, a separate low sits off the Pacific Northwest coast, and a diffuse quasi-stationary front lies east-west across Texas into the lower Mississippi Valley with mid-70s F dewpoints marking the main moisture axis. Over Canada, a dominant 1037 hPa high anchored on the central Shield near Hudson Bay drives a tight gradient down its western flank across the Prairies and into the northern Plains, while a late-stage cyclone of 992-996 hPa spins over the Great Lakes and southern Ontario.

Today's centers of action sit on the flanks of the block. On the fast southeastern flank of the Great Basin cutoff, SPC carries a Slight risk from eastern Nevada into northern Utah and across the northern Rockies and northern Plains, where high-based storms in enhanced mid-level flow over a deeply mixed boundary layer favor damaging wind gusts and hail. The Air Mass RGB shows a compact cluster of very cold tops near the Illinois-Indiana border with dry air wrapping cyclonically around it — an organized upper-level circulation or MCS tied to the surface low — and WPC extends that convection eastward to the Great Lakes, Mid-Atlantic, and New England through Monday. The Southwest stays in the monsoon under a Slight risk for excessive rainfall, with flash-flood potential over burn scars, slot canyons, and urban areas. Along the Gulf Coast and Southeast, storms fire on the moist side of the decaying stationary front, and NHC flags a weak low near Pensacola drifting west with a low (10 percent) development chance. The durable element, as it has been for days, is heat: the CPC 6-10 day outlook bullseyes 90-100 percent above-normal temperature odds over Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana, and Mississippi.

The upper pattern ties it together. The quasi-stationary cutoff and the fast flow curving around its eastern side are what drive the northern-stream severe corridor, while the eastern ridge blocks downstream progression. The RGB draws a sharp airmass boundary from the central Plains through the Tennessee Valley toward the Carolinas — dry orange air against green tropical moisture — which marks the implied jet corridor and coincides with the trailing surface front. The broad, saturated orange over the southern Plains, Desert Southwest, and northern Mexico is warm, subsiding, low-PV air under the summer ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion; that subsidence, mid-level warming, capping, and dry-air entrainment — not the modest 1017 mb Gulf high or the Desert Southwest surface high — is the mechanism building and maintaining the heat.

On the regime question, the ensemble pair points to geographic contraction rather than breakdown, and hints at a subtle westward shift from recent days. The 6-10 day chart (Sep 3-7) throws its strongest positive anomaly far north over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago and northern Hudson Bay, keeps the western North American ridge axis offshore-to-BC with its zero line cutting the interior Pacific Northwest so no positive anomaly reaches the western CONUS, and leaves a broad but modest negative anomaly over the central and southern states. The 8-14 day chart (Sep 5-11) then retreats the ridge core southwestward into the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico, deepens a closed low over the Arctic, keeps western Canada and the northern Rockies near-zero to weakly negative, and relocates the downstream positive anomaly offshore to the western Atlantic. WPC's days 3-7 discussion bridges the two, with a sprawling ridge from the central/southern Plains to the Southeast spreading Major-to-Extreme heat next week, even as a potent shortwave/closed low crossing southern Canada begins to force troughing over the Eastern U.S. Read across the pair: the heat ridge sprawls across the south-central and southeastern U.S. in the near term, then pulls back toward the Southwest and northern Mexico while the East transitions to troughing and the northern tier stays under the Canadian trough — a southwestward contraction and reload, not a collapse, and this cycle the core retreats farther southwest than the south-central reload of the past several days.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America near the seasonal script. The North American Monsoon remains active, feeding daily high-terrain convection across the Southwest and Rockies, and severe weather is expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream and frontal storms rather than deep-Plains outbreaks. The ENSO backdrop is a developing warm state: the ONI has climbed from weak La Niña late in 2025 through neutral in early 2026 toward roughly +1.39 by May-July 2026, i.e. a developing moderate El Niño. That configuration typically favors an active eastern Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and even as the basin nears its early-September climatological peak the tropics fit that split — the East Pacific holds Tropical Storms Karina and Lowell, a 90 percent development area south of southwestern Mexico, a compact cyclonic feature off the Mexican coast, and a 40 percent area near Hawaii, against a quiet Atlantic where Dolly's remnants and a weak Gulf low each carry only a 10 percent chance over seven days. Summer teleconnection indices lag the height field and are best used as cross-checks: a deepening negative PNA (7-day mean -0.70) is loosely consistent with the western trough and the eastern troughing the ensembles build later next week, and a sharply negative NAO (7-day mean -0.90, now -1.28) is directionally supportive of the high-latitude blocking implied by the Canadian Arctic positive anomaly — supporting, not the basis for, the story the height and PV fields already tell.
