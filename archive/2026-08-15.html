Synoptic Picture

The south-central heat ridge holds but flattens as its western trough weakens; the day's storminess piles onto the ridge's north and east flanks, and the medium range keeps retreating the ridge toward the Southwest and northern Mexico

The amplified summer dipole Tlaloc has tracked for well over a week runs on, but with a notable softening in one half of it. The southern heat ridge is intact — this morning's 500 mb analysis puts a broad, relatively flat ridge over the south-central and southeastern US with heights near 594 dam along the Gulf Coast — while the western trough that was deep and near-cutoff yesterday has flattened into a long, low-amplitude, open trough from the Pacific Northwest through the Great Basin, not meeting blocking criteria. The center of gravity of the day's weather has correspondingly shifted east: the eastern-stream disturbance has matured into a well-developed extratropical cyclone with a comma-head and deep convection over the Ohio Valley and interior Mid-Atlantic. At the surface, a strong high dominates the central High Plains (the WPC analyst flags the plotted 1046 mb as almost certainly a misread — likely 1026-1036 — so treat the exact value with caution), a weak low sits over the central southern Plains, and Gulf dewpoints in the mid-70s pool the richest moisture across the lower Mississippi Valley and Gulf Coast. Canada carries real weight today: a broad ridge covers the western provinces while a deep 993 hPa low over Quebec/the Gulf of St. Lawrence drags a trailing cold front south with a tight gradient and northwesterly flow into Ontario and the northeastern US.

The centers of action still ring the ridge's northern and western rims, with the eastern cyclone now added on top. The most dangerous story remains the heat: WPC keeps a locked-in, multi-day hazardous heat wave over the southern tier, spreading into the Carolinas by Monday with heat indices near 115 and Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk — its convective suppression coming from midlevel subsidence and warming, a strong cap and dry air aloft, not from any surface high. Along the ridge's north flank a heavy-rain, severe, and flash-flood corridor runs from the Midwest through the central Appalachians and central High Plains, with a Moderate Risk for excessive rainfall over the Midwest/Ohio Valley Saturday-Sunday and West Virginia Sunday. SPC's Slight Risk covers the central High Plains (supercells and multicell lines off the Rockies, wind and hail) and the middle Ohio Valley (wet microbursts as a vort max reignites afternoon convection). Monsoonal moisture keeps a separate flash-flood threat going across the Interior West and Four Corners. Offshore, a compact, apparently independent convective cluster sits over the western Atlantic off the Carolinas, distinct from the continental system, though a single frame can't establish its organization.

The upper-level structure ties it together. The most prominent feature on the wind field is a 95-115 kt jet streak sweeping from the upper Midwest through the Great Lakes into the Northeast, curving anticyclonically toward New England — this is what is translating the eastern trough and its cyclone downstream and keeping the East progressive. The Air Mass RGB shows the ridge's signature: a broad red-orange plume of dry, subsident mid/upper-tropospheric air over the southern Plains, Gulf, and Southeast (warm-season ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion), with the sharp airmass boundary from the central Plains into the Great Lakes marking the implied jet corridor and baroclinic zone under the convective shield. A compact, intense reddish signature near the Colorado/New Mexico border reads as a possible localized dry intrusion wrapping into a shortwave — consistent with the small closed contour over Wyoming and the High Plains storm threat, though its scale warrants caution. On the regime question — whether the heat ridge is transitioning or merely being nudged — the two ensemble leads answer consistently and reinforce the last several days' message. The 6-10 day (Aug 20-24) puts its zero anomaly line through roughly 110-100°W, keeps the western CONUS near neutral, runs the positive-anomaly axis from the north-central Pacific into Alaska/BC, and flattens the East toward zonal. The 8-14 day (Aug 21-27) then retreats the ridge core even farther south and southwest, bulging the 588 dam contour across the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico with positive anomalies concentrated there and off Baja, while western Canada and the northern Rockies show no ridge and the East lies under a broad neutral-to-weakly-negative zone beneath a downstream trough. That is a genuine geographic contraction and southward retreat of the ridge toward the Southwest and northern Mexico, not a collapse and not a rebuild over the north — and both leads now agree on a deep cold low over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, resolving the high-latitude disagreement flagged yesterday.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America running close to the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon is active across the Southwest and southern Rockies, and severe-weather season is being expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream convection along a wavy frontal zone rather than classic Plains supercells. The ENSO backdrop is a developing-to-established warm state — the ONI has climbed out of last year's weak La Niña (near -0.6) through neutral late winter into decisively warm territory, reaching +1.39 by May-July 2026, indicating a developing El Niño. That configuration typically favors the East Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and today's tropics fit the split as the basin nears its early-September peak: the East Pacific is busy with Tropical Storm Lala east-southeast of Hawaii, Tropical Depression Hernan off Baja, and two disturbances each given 30% over seven days, while the Atlantic stays quiet, with a southeastern-Caribbean wave expected to stay disorganized and a central-Atlantic wave at just 10%, both fighting strong upper winds and dry air. Teleconnections are only a directional cross-check in summer and are mixed today — the NAO has turned distinctly negative (-0.90), which would favor eastern blocking and is broadly consistent with the amplified downstream trough, but the AO has swung positive (+0.37), stepping away from high-latitude blocking, and the PNA is weakly negative; the summer weakness of these indices warrants caution in leaning on any one of them.
