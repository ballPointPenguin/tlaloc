Synoptic Picture

Fall dipole winds down: Nor'easter exits into the Gulf of Maine as a Polo-fed heavy-rain regime organizes in the West — and the ensembles keep the mid-latitude western ridge from rebuilding, retreating its influence to the Southwest while the Arctic amplifies

The autumn longwave dipole that has framed the continent all week is still the organizing story, but both anchors are now maturing and the center of gravity is shifting to the middle of the map. At the eastern end, the coastal cyclone tracked for days — near 990 mb this morning — is pulling from the Mid-Atlantic into the Gulf of Maine as a strong Nor'easter, still coupled to a near-cutoff 500 mb low over the mid-Atlantic (heights near 572-575 dam) that will meander rather than translate cleanly. At the western end, a deep, amplified 500 mb trough sits over the Pacific Northwest and northern Rockies (core near 552 dam) with a slightly negative tilt as its base swings into the Great Basin; at the surface it appears as a sprawling low/trough complex across the Intermountain West and central Rockies with fronts draped south into the Plains and Desert Southwest. Between the two, the gradient over the interior is slack, so the headline threat for the coming days is not synoptic wind but tropical-moisture-fed rainfall. Canada carries real weight today too: a deep 981 hPa occluded low over interior BC/southern Yukon drives a tight onshore-and-inland gradient along the BC coast, while a broad 1034 hPa high over Hudson Bay and the eastern Shield ridges cool air southward into the upper Great Lakes.

Today's centers of action: first, the departing Nor'easter, with coastal flooding, rip currents, 40-50 mph gusts and heavy rain through Monday, and a WPC Slight Risk for excessive rainfall over southern New England into Monday. Second, and increasingly the main event, a multi-day heavy-rain and flash-flood regime in the West: WPC hangs a moderate excessive-rainfall risk over the Southwest, Great Basin and central/southern Rockies Monday, expanding to the southern High Plains Tuesday, with the western closed low and its wavy fronts drifting slowly toward the South-Central U.S. later in the week — a threat fed directly by tropical moisture from East Pacific Hurricane Polo (poised to landfall in Mexico) layered onto deep Gulf inflow. Third, a northern-Plains low will spread a Slight Risk for excessive rainfall into the Upper Midwest and Middle Mississippi Valley by Tuesday. Severe weather is a minor note: only a Marginal Risk over the central/northern High Plains for large hail. And offshore Mexico, Polo is both a coastal-impact threat and the moisture pipeline pointed at the Four Corners.

Upstairs, the Air Mass RGB is consistent with a broad dry upper airmass over the western two-thirds of the CONUS tied to the ridge over the Plains and Southwest (a 500 mb high near 592 dam over the north-central Plains/upper Midwest, and a subtropical ridge near 591-592 dam over the southern Plains and northern Mexico), while the sharpest airmass boundary — the implied jet corridor — arcs from the upper Midwest through the Tennessee Valley to the Southeast coast, separating dry air from deep tropical green. A compact high-PV dry streamer curls cyclonically around the exiting Northeast trough, a credible stratospheric-intrusion signature on its cyclonic flank; note the imagery shows dry, high-PV air, not descent per se. The regime question is where today matters most. Comparing the two ensemble leads directly: the 6-10 day (Oct 2-6) holds the dominant positive height anomaly offshore over the central/eastern North Pacific with the zero line near or just west of the Rockies and a broadly zonal, progressive CONUS — no inland ridge to anchor. The 8-14 day (Oct 4-10) then shrinks the mid-latitude western ridge further and relocates its influence southward: the strongest positive anomalies (+30 to +40 dam) sit over the Four Corners and northern Mexico, western Canada and the northern Rockies go transitional-to-weakly-negative, and a deeply amplified polar low anchors over the Canadian Arctic near Baffin. WPC's extended discussion reinforces this precisely — a southern-stream split-flow closed low crawling from the Southwest to the South-Central U.S., blocked between separate ridge developments over the Southwest and Southeast, with a progressive, zonal northern stream firing frontal systems across the northern tier. This is the picture yesterday's analysis pointed to, now firmer: the durable western ridge is not regenerating over the lower 48. Rather than collapsing outright it is contracting geographically — retreating toward the Southwest and northern Mexico while the north cools and the true amplification moves poleward to the Arctic. That is a genuine regime transition, not a one-shortwave flattening.

Climate Context

The ONI table's "current" cool label lags its own data: the equatorial Pacific climbed out of last winter's weak La Niña, crossed positive in spring, and reaches roughly +1.80 by JJA 2026, placing late September in a moderate-to-strong El Niño. The tropics fit that state and the just-past hurricane-season peak with a textbook basin split. The Atlantic is comparatively quiet — Tropical Depression Fay near the Azores and a low-probability trough east of Bermuda (40 percent through seven days), both no near-term continental concern — while the East Pacific is hyperactive, running four systems at once (Hurricanes Polo, Odalys and Nolo plus Tropical Depression Eighteen-E) with another likely depression east-southeast of Hawaii at 80 percent through a week. The North American Monsoon is in its fading late-season phase but still supplies the Pacific-and-Gulf moisture overlap that, joined to Polo's tropical connection, will fuel the Southwest-to-South-Central rains into the coming week. Late-season severe weather remains in its expected ridge-periphery, southern-Plains mode rather than deep-Plains outbreaks. Teleconnections are only a weak warm-season cross-check but align with the height field: the modestly negative PNA is consistent with the absence of a mid-latitude western ridge, and the negative NAO (7-day mean near -1.01, -0.66 today) supports the stalling eastern cutoff and the emerging high-latitude Arctic amplification.
