Synoptic Picture

The fall block breaks in the West: a deep Northwest trough replaces the coastal ridge while the eastern cutoff couples with an intensifying coastal storm — and the Southwest flood ridge retreats south rather than collapsing

The high-amplitude, blocked longwave pattern that has organized the continent for roughly five days is now visibly transitioning at both ends while its southern anchor holds. In the West, the Pacific-coast ridge that stood yesterday has been replaced by a deep 500 mb trough over the Pacific Northwest and northern Rockies (heights near or below 564 dam), and at the surface a strong 1037 mb high has settled over the north-central Plains and upper Midwest — the cooler continental airmass the ensembles have been advertising, now arrived and driving northerly flow into the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley. In the East, yesterday's Ohio Valley cutoff has slid to the mid-Atlantic coast, where a slightly negatively tilted closed low sits roughly over the Delmarva region, cut off from the westerlies and quasi-stationary. The subtropical ridge over the Gulf Coast and Southeast (588 dam) still bridges the southern tier, so the meridional, blocky character of the pattern persists even as its western and eastern lobes evolve.

Today's centers of action are the coastal storm and the Southwest flood axis. The mid-Atlantic cutoff is coupling with an intensifying offshore coastal low; after trending weaker earlier in the week, guidance now brings a genuinely impactful system, with WPC advertising wind gusts over 45 mph, moderate-to-major coastal flooding, high surf and heavy rain from the Mid-Atlantic into southern New England and eastern Massachusetts, persisting through the weekend and meandering near the Northeast into early next week. Placement remains the key uncertainty — the ECMWF is a westward, sub-980 mb outlier near the New Jersey coast, but the consensus keeps the low south to southeast of Long Island. The second focus is the Southwest and southern/central Plains, where the upper trough over Arizona and deep tropical moisture keep a Slight Risk of excessive rainfall over New Mexico into the Plains with flash flooding expected Friday as the moisture axis lifts northeast into the Midwest; Hurricane Polo, a large and near-peak system on the full-disk imagery off western Mexico, remains the plausible moisture pipeline. A third, quieter focus is the Pacific Northwest, where the deepening trough brings widespread showers to Washington, Oregon and the northern Rockies with high-elevation snow by Friday.

Upstairs, the Air Mass RGB ties the surface story together: cold, dry high-latitude air (purple-blue) is banked over the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia behind the western trough, a sharp airmass boundary runs from the central Plains southeast into the western Gulf marking the implied jet corridor, and a cyclonically curving dry streamer of higher-PV air wraps the eastern flank of the Rockies trough into Oklahoma and northern Texas — a credible dry intrusion feeding the Southwest system. The regime-change question is best settled by the ensemble height charts, and they agree: both the 6-10 and 8-14 day means keep a modestly amplified ridge (+40 to +60 m) over the Desert Southwest and northern Mexico, but by the 8-14 day period the ridge core has retreated to the Four Corners with no meaningful positive anomaly over western Canada, which is undercut by a deepening Arctic trough over Baffin Island. This is geographic contraction and a southward retreat, not a breakdown — the northern block genuinely cools and the storm track displaces into the upper Midwest and Great Lakes, while the Southwest ridge endures as a durable feature. WPC's extended discussion underscores the near-term uncertainty: models split on whether next week's western trough phases through or splits, with a southern-stream Four Corners upper low that could tap Polo's moisture and reload the Southwest wet pattern.

The tropics fit that state cleanly just past the climatological hurricane-season peak, showing the textbook basin split — the Atlantic is comparatively quiet (Tropical Storm Fay near the Azores and a Cabo Verde-area wave given a 70 percent chance before shear caps it this weekend), while the East Pacific is crowded with Hurricane Polo near southwestern Mexico, Hurricane Odalys west-southwest of Baja, Tropical Storm Nolo south of Hawaii, and an 80 percent development area south of the Gulf of Tehuantepec. Polo is the direct moisture source under scrutiny for the Southwest flood event. Seasonally, the North American Monsoon corridor remains the venue where Pacific and Gulf moisture overlap over New Mexico and the southern Rockies, and late-season severe weather is running in its expected ridge-periphery, northern-tier mode rather than as deep-Plains outbreaks. Teleconnections are only a weak warm-season cross-check but line up with the height field: a sharply negative NAO (7-day mean near -1.01) favoring high-latitude Atlantic blocking is consistent with the mid-Atlantic cutoff stalling rather than escaping downstream, while the weak-negative PNA fits the eastern troughing and the volatile, near-neutral AO carries little weight.
