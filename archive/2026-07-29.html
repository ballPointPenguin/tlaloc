Synoptic Picture

The summer block, now weeks deep, delivers its fully cut-off eastern low and a record-flirting western heat dome — but the ridge shows its first crack next week

The high-amplitude summer block Tlaloc has tracked for roughly three weeks remains the single organizing story, and today it reaches the mature stage yesterday's analysis anticipated: the eastern trough has fully closed off. The 500 mb analysis places a cutoff low near the Virginia/Pennsylvania border with anomalously cold cores of -10 to -16C aloft, anchoring a negatively tilted longwave trough that runs from the Great Lakes southwest to the central Gulf Coast. Opposite it, a large-amplitude ridge capped by the 594 dm contour dominates the Rockies and High Plains in a Rex-block-like configuration. The pattern is blocked, not progressive, so both extremes stay put: a slow, soaking East and a baking, expanding West. At the surface this manifests as a 1002 mb low over the central Appalachians and mid-Atlantic — the same feature Air Mass RGB shows as a vigorous cyclonic system with a cold-top convective core just off the Carolina Outer Banks, curling toward New England — while a broad ridge (1018-1020 mb) suppresses convection across the western interior.

Today's centers of action ring the block's rim. The eastern cutoff is the busiest: WPC carries a Moderate Risk of excessive rainfall across the Northeast for Wednesday from frontal convergence, anomalous moisture, and training, with the flash-flood threat continuing Thursday. Farther south along the stalling cold front, SPC's Slight Risk covers the Southeast, where dewpoints in the 70s and MLCAPE of 3000+ J/kg support damaging-wind clusters by evening, and Marginal-to-Slight excessive-rainfall risks overlap the same slow-moving storms. A second severe focus sits on the block's northern rim, where an approaching disturbance and lee trough fire storms across the western Dakotas (large hail, severe gusts) with a shortwave over the northern Intermountain West adding scattered severe potential from Idaho into northern Utah. Beneath the ridge, a dangerous heat wave with indices of 105-115 expands from the South into the Southwest and Intermountain West, and the North American Monsoon continues afternoon storms and burn-scar flash-flood concerns across the Four Corners and Rockies. Canada carries real weight: a dominant 1037 hPa high over the Prairies keeps that region quiet, a Pacific cold front drives southeastward along the BC coast toward the northern Rockies, and a 1004-1008 hPa Great Lakes low pushes a warm front into Ontario and a cold front into the upper Midwest, keeping the Ontario-Quebec border zone active.

Aloft the block explains everything cleanly. A prominent red-orange PV streamer — high-PV stratospheric air descending on the anticyclonic flank of the ridge — arcs from the northern Plains through the Tennessee Valley into the Gulf Coast, and the sharpest airmass boundary from Missouri through the Carolinas marks the implied jet axis, separating vivid tropical green to the southeast from the dry stratospheric wedge to the northwest. That jet-and-PV structure is what wraps energy into the mid-Atlantic cyclone and keeps storm motions slow enough to compound the flash-flood threat. Crucially, the WPC extended discussion flags the first crack in the block: a strong closed low dropping into southwestern Canada late this week will begin to shrink the western ridge and drive a cold front through the Pacific Northwest and northern Plains by the weekend, while the eastern trough amplifies southward and keeps the East wet into early next week. The ridge, however, holds longest and hottest over the Desert Southwest and Texas, where the extended range points to widespread 110s, some lower 120s in the hottest spots, and numerous daily records — the cold front simply will not reach that far south.

Climate Context

This is textbook late-July North America. The North American Monsoon is in full swing over the Desert Southwest and southern Rockies, feeding both the flash-flood and localized severe threats, while peak heat-and-severe season drives the entrenched interior heat dome and the marginal severe threats along the trough's periphery; the CPC 6-10 day outlook keeps a 90-100 percent above-normal bullseye over the Great Basin and Desert Southwest, so the western ridge only shrinks at its northern edge rather than breaking down through early August. The hurricane basins remain split against the climatological ramp toward peak season: the Atlantic, Caribbean, and Gulf are quiet with no development expected over the next seven days, while the East Pacific stays busy with Tropical Depression Fausto now in the central Pacific northeast of Hawaii and Hurricane Genevieve southwest of Baja California. The ENSO backdrop is one of steady cold-to-warm transition — after a weak La Nina near -0.5C, the ONI has warmed through neutral toward warm-phase territory by late spring, a shift that will increasingly shape steering and shear through the back half of the Atlantic season.
