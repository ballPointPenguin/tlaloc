Synoptic Picture

The week-long western block finally cedes: heat contracts to the southern Plains while an eastern trough sweeps severe into the Southeast and a Gulf cutoff spins up

The high-amplitude, blocked longwave pattern Tlaloc has tracked all week is still recognizable this morning, but it is now visibly winding down rather than holding. The DuPage 500 mb analysis keeps the familiar three-part backbone — a strongly amplified ridge over the central Plains (588-594 dam), a deep western trough, and an eastern trough anchored over the Great Lakes — but two things have shifted. First, the western feature is no longer a compact Pacific Northwest cutoff sitting east of an anticyclone; it has become a broad, high-amplitude longwave trough whose axis runs down the Pacific coast from British Columbia into the Desert Southwest, with a slight negative tilt in its southern reaches. Second, a genuinely new player has appeared: a well-defined closed low cut off over the central Gulf of Mexico (roughly 580-582 dam at its center), with a separate secondary closed circulation near the Texas-Louisiana coast. The organizing story today is therefore transition — the western ridging that dominated the week is giving way, the heat is consolidating over the southern tier, and the Gulf has spun up a slow-pivoting cutoff of its own.

Today's action lines up, as it has all week, on the flanks. The primary severe threat rides the eastern trough: an MCS coming out of the Ohio Valley (Watch 655 over northeast Indiana and central Ohio overnight) reintensifies as it drives into the central Appalachians and Mid-Atlantic this afternoon, with damaging wind the main concern and the best shear/buoyancy overlap over eastern North Carolina. The Air Mass RGB flags an elongated, curved cold-cloud shield with an implied circulation off the Delmarva/New Jersey coast worth watching. Behind the southward-advancing front, cool air pours into the Northeast (mid-70s to low-80s by Sunday, frost possible in the higher elevations). Under the contracting ridge, heat indices of 105-110°F continue across the Central and Southern Plains and Mississippi Valley — this is where the durable signal lives. On the heavy-rain side, a Slight Risk for excessive rainfall covers southern Nevada into Utah Sunday as monsoon moisture runs ahead of the slow-moving western front, and a consolidating low over eastern Montana and the western Dakotas ramps up northern Plains storms late in the weekend. In Canada, a stout 1034 hPa high over the western Hudson Bay/Canadian Shield sets up the tightest domestic gradient from the Prairies into Ontario.

The upper-level context ties the transition together and answers the regime question directly. The broad orange-red plume across the central and southern CONUS on the Air Mass RGB is warm, dry, low-PV subsidence air under the ridge — the heat and convective suppression there come from midlevel subsidence, warming, and capping, not from the modest surface highs beneath it, and the sharper dry streamer curling from the central Plains toward Texas and northern Mexico marks the jet on the eastern flank of the western trough. The ensemble pair confirms this is a real retreat, not a cosmetic flattening. The 6-10 day (Sep 10-14) height field pulls the positive-anomaly center up to central Canada near 80-90°W with the zero line already near the Pacific coast — no meaningful western-CONUS ridging left. By 8-14 days (Sep 12-18) that high-latitude Canadian ridge collapses outright into a deep polar low (heights near 528 dam) with a broad 60-90 m negative anomaly across western and central Canada, and the only surviving positive anomaly has retreated to the southern Plains, Gulf Coast, and northern Mexico. That is the classic ridge-retreat-toward-the-south signature carried to its conclusion: the western and northern ridging is gone from the maps, the southern tier stays hot (the CPC 6-10 temperature outlook plants a 90-100% above-normal bull's-eye on the Oklahoma-Texas border), and the northern tier trends cooler and more progressive. The SPC Day 2 discussion already shows the mechanism starting Sunday, with the Plains ridge "weakening and shifting east" as a western shortwave digs toward the northern Rockies. The falling, now solidly negative NAO (7-day mean near -1.0) is directionally consistent with the eastern troughing and the Gulf cutoff behavior, but as a weak, lagging summer signal it is a cross-check on the height field, not the basis for it.

Climate Context

This is peak-season North America running close to script under a developing El Niño. The ONI table shows the basin having flipped from weak La Niña (about -0.6°C late 2025) to a forecast strong El Niño by summer 2026 (+1.80 in JJA), and the tropical field fits that state precisely: a crowded East Pacific carrying three simultaneous hurricanes (Lowell and Karina near Hawaii, Marie west of southern Baja) plus a 60% chance of another system developing offshore southwestern Mexico late next week, set against a suppressed Atlantic where — at the climatological hurricane-season peak — no development is expected anywhere for seven days. The North American Monsoon remains active, driving the Southwest excessive-rainfall risk into Nevada and Utah. Severe weather is expressing itself in the expected late-summer mode — ridge-periphery and frontal storms across the Great Lakes, Ohio Valley, Appalachians, and now the Southeast rather than deep-Plains outbreaks — while the dominant domestic climate signal remains the durable central-U.S. heat, now consolidating over the south-central Plains as the western block retreats.
