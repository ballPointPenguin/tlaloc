Synoptic Picture

The summer dipole runs on: southern heat ridge re-sharpened, western trough deepened, and the medium range keeps retreating the ridge toward the Southwest and northern Mexico

The amplified warm-season dipole Tlaloc has tracked for well over a week is still the organizing story, and today the western half has firmed back up after yesterday's flattening. The 12Z 500 mb analysis puts a broad, relatively flat subtropical ridge across the southern tier with heights near 594-597 dam along the Gulf Coast and an anticyclonic center off the Louisiana-Mississippi coast, while a longwave western trough runs from the Pacific Northwest down through the Great Basin and interior West with heights near 583-589 dam and a slight negative tilt on its southern end. There is no closed cutoff — the western trough is open to the westerlies — so this is an amplified but progressive pattern, not a block. At the surface the geometry matches: coherent 1026 mb highs anchor the northern High Plains/Montana and the central Great Basin, storm tracks are shoved north, and a modest 1015-1016 mb low near the Texas Panhandle anchors a frontal boundary draped east-northeast into the mid-Mississippi Valley, along which Gulf dewpoints in the upper 60s to low 70s pool the day's richest moisture. Canada carries real weight again: a broad 1026 hPa ridge covers the western provinces while a 1003-1004 hPa low with fronts is draped across the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence, and a newly analyzed low near the prairies/northern Plains border is pushing an active boundary eastward.

The centers of action ring the ridge's northern and eastern rims. The most dangerous element remains the heat: WPC sustains a locked-in, multi-day dangerous heat wave across the South and Southeast under the subtropical ridge, with heat indices near 115 and widespread Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk through the week — its convective suppression coming from midlevel subsidence and warming, a strong cap and dry air aloft, not from any surface high. Along the ridge's north and east flanks a heavy-rain and severe corridor is active: GOES Air Mass RGB shows a compact deep-convective cluster near the Nebraska-Kansas border and a second comma-like convective mass over the mid-Atlantic and interior Northeast consistent with a maturing synoptic cyclone. WPC carries a Moderate Excessive Rainfall Risk on West Virginia today with flash-flood potential also across the central High Plains, and SPC's Day 1 Slight Risk covers the Ohio Valley, central Appalachians, mid-Atlantic and central High Plains for damaging wind, isolated hail and a low tornado risk (notably northern Virginia). This corridor holds into Monday: the Day 2 outlook keeps Slight Risks over Nebraska/the mid-Missouri Valley — where 2500-3500 J/kg MLCAPE and 35+ kt effective shear support supercells with large hail and, if upscale growth occurs, a greater wind threat — and over the south-central Appalachians.

The upper structure ties it together. A modest westerly jet corridor at roughly 570 dam runs across the northern Plains and Great Lakes at 30-50 kt, and Day 2 guidance has a shortwave deepening east-southeast across Ontario and the Upper Midwest, driving the height falls that will overspread the northern Plains with northwesterly flow — this is what keeps the northern-stream convection going and translates the eastern cyclone downstream. The Air Mass RGB shows the ridge's signature red-orange plume of dry, subsident upper-tropospheric air over the Gulf, Southeast and southern Plains (a warm-season ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion), with a sharper cyclonically-curved dry streamer from the southern Rockies into the central Plains marking the abrupt airmass boundary and implied jet corridor on the equatorward flank of the western trough. On the regime question — whether the heat ridge is transitioning or merely being nudged — the two ensemble leads answer consistently and reinforce the past several days' message. The 6-10 day (Aug 21-25) closes 594 dam over the Desert Southwest but keeps the zero-anomaly line near 100-105°W and the positive-anomaly center no farther north than 40-45°N, so the western ridge holds in subtropical latitudes without surging poleward; the Pacific Northwest and interior Canada stay outside it, and the East flattens toward zonal. The 8-14 day (Aug 21-27 window) then places the ridge core over the Four Corners and northern Mexico with the northern Rockies and western Canada in a neutral-to-weakly-negative zone. Read against each other, the pair shows a genuine geographic contraction and southward retreat of the ridge toward the Southwest and northern Mexico — not a collapse and not a rebuild over the north — while both leads agree on a deep cold low anchored over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago as the most amplified feature on the continent.

In the northwestern Atlantic off Atlantic Canada, a well-organized mid-latitude cyclone shows a textbook spiral with a high-PV dry intrusion wrapping its bent-back region; over eastern Canada the Great Lakes/St. Lawrence low and the newly analyzed prairie-border low drive the active boundaries affecting Ontario and Quebec.

Climate Context

This is peak-summer North America running close to the seasonal script: the North American Monsoon supports the southwestern moisture, and severe-weather season is being expressed as ridge-periphery, northern-stream convection along a wavy frontal zone rather than classic deep-Plains supercells. The ENSO backdrop is a developing warm state — the ONI has climbed out of last year's weak La Niña (OND 2025 near -0.61) through neutral in late winter into decisively warm territory, reaching about +1.39 by late spring 2026, indicating a transition toward El Niño. That configuration typically favors the East Pacific while shear and dry air suppress the Atlantic, and today's tropics fit the split as the basin approaches its early-September peak: the Atlantic is quiet with no development expected in seven days, while the East Pacific hosts Tropical Storm Lala south of Honolulu plus two low-pressure areas (one south of Mexico, one southeast of Hawaii) each near 50% over the week. Teleconnections are only a directional cross-check in summer and are mixed today — the NAO has swung strongly negative (-1.28, -0.90 mean), which would nominally favor eastern troughing/cutoff potential, but the AO has flipped positive (+0.37), stepping away from high-latitude blocking, and the PNA is weakly negative, consistent with diminished western ridging; given the weak reliability of these indices in summer, they are used here only to corroborate a story the height and PV fields already tell.
