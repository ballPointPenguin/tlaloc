Synoptic Picture

The three-week block finally reconfigures: a cold cutoff drops into the northern Rockies while the Southwest heat dome digs in for the long haul

The high-amplitude summer block Tlaloc has tracked for roughly three weeks is not resetting, but it is visibly reorganizing — and today marks the turn yesterday's analysis anticipated. Yesterday the block was defined by a fully cut-off low over the Mid-Atlantic and a heat ridge parked over the Rockies. Today those pieces have moved: at 500 mb the eastern cutoff has slid offshore, now just a negatively tilted shortwave and PV streamer curling over southern New England, while a deep, unusually cold cutoff low has dropped onto the Montana-Wyoming border (core heights near 5720 dm). The ridge that governed the West has retreated and elongated from the Gulf of Alaska into central Canada, leaving a classic Rex-block couplet — cold vortex to the south, ridge to the north — that keeps western flow stalled. The practical result is a two-story continent: a hot, largely quiet interior West under the surviving ridge, and an active northern and eastern rim where the new energy is working.

Today's centers of action ring that rim. The busiest is the central Plains, where SPC carries a Slight Risk across Nebraska and South Dakota: a shortwave ejecting from the Wyoming cutoff meets a moisture-rich airmass and a southeastward-sagging cold front, with early supercells (large hail) expected to congeal into a damaging-wind cluster this evening as the low-level jet ramps up. Downstream, WPC flags a Slight Risk of excessive rainfall over the Mid to Lower Missouri and Mississippi Valleys, where training storms along the same front raise the flash-flood threat. To the southeast, deep convection is already firing from Louisiana through Georgia and into Florida along the stalled front and the moist-dry airmass boundary, anchored by a weak 1008 mb Gulf Coast low, while the departing Northeast low keeps a lingering flash-flood risk over New England through Thursday. Beneath the ridge, dangerous heat dominates the South and is set to expand into the Southwest, Intermountain West, and northern High Plains, with heat indices of 105-115 and Major-to-Extreme HeatRisk. Canada carries equal weight today: a deep 979 mb low over Hudson Bay drives gusty northwest flow across Ontario, Quebec, and Atlantic Canada against a 1037 mb Atlantic high, while a 1028 mb ridge keeps British Columbia and Alberta settled and a secondary low over the northern Prairies focuses convection along the Manitoba-Saskatchewan border — the northern extension of the same frontal system trailing into the U.S. Plains.

Aloft the structure ties it together. The equatorward flank of the northern-Rockies cutoff hosts a jet streak exceeding 105 knots oriented southwest-to-northeast across the northern Plains, and its negative tilt maximizes dynamic lift over the Upper Midwest — precisely where the severe and heavy-rain threats are concentrated. Air Mass RGB shows the reason in color: a broad orange-red stratospheric dry intrusion sweeps from the northern Rockies through the High Plains into Texas on the trough's back side, with the sharpest boundary against vivid tropical green — the implied jet axis — running from the Upper Midwest southwestward through Kansas and Oklahoma. A second, tighter PV filament over the Northeast marks the closing-off eastern low. Looking ahead, WPC's extended discussion confirms the block breaks down only at its northern edge: a closed low and trailing cold front work inland across southwestern Canada this weekend into Monday, cooling the Pacific Northwest and northern Plains, while the Southwest ridge holds longest and hottest with widespread 110s and record warm overnight lows persisting into next week.

This is textbook late-July North America. The North American Monsoon is in full swing over the Desert Southwest and southern Rockies, feeding the Four Corners flash-flood and localized severe threats, while peak heat-and-severe season drives both the entrenched interior-West heat dome and the marginal severe threats along the trough's periphery; the CPC 6-10 day outlook keeps a 90-100 percent above-normal bullseye over the Great Basin and interior Southwest, confirming the western ridge only erodes at its northern edge rather than collapsing through early August.
