Synoptic Picture

The week-long cutoff hands off: a maturing southern-Plains trough slides toward a Gulf/Southeast flood regime while the northern stream takes charge — and the western ridge that looked finished yesterday reloads in week two

The organizing story is a two-stream handoff. The amplified, blocked couplet Tlaloc has tracked all week is now entering its exit phase: at 500 mb the once-discrete interior-West cutoff has broadened into a deep central-US longwave trough, nearly cut off with a height minimum of roughly 540-546 dam over the southern Plains and a slight negative (NW-SE) tilt from the northern Rockies southeast. That is the same slow, pivoting system in roughly its fourth day, still walled off enough to creep rather than sweep. But the northern stream has woken up around it: a vigorous 1002-1004 mb surface cyclone has come onshore over the north-central Rockies/northern Great Basin — the Pacific disturbance yesterday's analysis flagged as the first to erode the ridge — trailing a cold front through the central Plains with the chart's tightest pressure gradient (and strongest winds) from the northern Plains south through the central Rockies. Behind it, a strong 1026 mb cold high over the PNW/northern Rockies and a 1024 mb center over the upper Midwest mark the fall airmass beginning to push south, reinforced from the north by a formidable 1039 hPa high over the Keewatin/western Hudson Bay region on the Canadian analysis.

Focal points, in order of consequence: first, heavy rain. WPC carries a Moderate Risk for excessive rainfall today over the Southern Plains as Gulf moisture pools ahead of the front, with flash-flood and burn-scar debris-flow concern, and a Slight Risk reaching the Central Plains, mid-Mississippi Valley and Great Lakes. Severe weather is a secondary note today — SPC keeps organized potential low, cloud cover and poor lapse rates capping destabilization, so repetitive heavy rain is the real threat and only gusty storms with small hail are likely near the mid-level low in the Panhandle/southern Kansas. Second, the Mid-Atlantic/southern New England coast, where the Air Mass RGB shows a tight airmass boundary with curving cirrus filaments consistent with a coastal cyclone or occlusion, with the broad 1021-1022 mb offshore high leaving a slack gradient down the seaboard. Third, a NE-SW convective band from Louisiana/Mississippi toward the Carolinas along the trailing boundary, plus a deep-convective cluster over the central Gulf — tropical-moisture players feeding the developing South/Southeast rain focus, not named threats (NHC expects no Atlantic development for seven days).

The upper pattern ties it together and sets up the week. The ridge over the Pacific Northwest and into southern Canada still reaches the upper 570s to near 580 dam, a genuinely amplified couplet with the trough to its east, and the implied jet wraps cyclonically from the Southwest northeastward toward the Great Lakes at 60-70+ kt. The Air Mass RGB confirms the mechanism: a dry streamer descending the eastern Rockies and High Plains through Texas and curving toward the Gulf marks the trough's western flank, its leading edge the ascent corridor into the moist plume driving the Southern Plains rain; the full-disk view shows a sharp color boundary across Canada marking the leading edge of the polar airmass now sagging south. Where today departs from yesterday's read is the medium range. Yesterday Tlaloc called the northern ridge genuinely transitioning — contracting and retreating toward the Southwest, with no rebuild. Today's ensemble pair tells a more nuanced story, and it matters to say so. The 6-10 day (Oct 6-10) does flatten the West: near-zero height anomalies, the zero line pulled back to the interior West near 110-115W, a progressive near-zonal CONUS flow, and the dominant positive anomaly displaced east to a +9 to +12 dam ridge over/west of Hudson Bay. But the 8-14 day (Oct 8-14) swings the other way — a strong +90 m ridge rebuilds over the Pacific Northwest, BC coast and northern Rockies (ridge core anchored there, not retreated to the Southwest), while central Canada/Hudson Bay flips from that 6-10 day ridge to a deep trough. Read as a pair, this looks less like a clean regime change and more like a temporary progressive flattening — the train of northern-stream fronts passing through — followed by re-amplification of a northern-tier western ridge. WPC's extended discussion supports that framing explicitly, describing northern-stream amplification over time behind the progressive fronts. The honest caveat: the two panels disagree on where the dominant ridge lives (Hudson Bay at 6-10, PNW at 8-14), so confidence in the week-2 placement is only moderate, but the signal is regeneration of western ridging, not its demise.

Climate Context

The basin behavior is textbook moderate-to-strong El Niño: the ONI table, though lagging at its labeled value, climbs to about +1.8 by JJA 2026, placing early October squarely in El Niño territory, and the hemispheric tropics fit that state — a hyperactive East Pacific (Hurricane Rachel south of Baja California Sur, Tropical Storm Nolo west of Hawaii, Tropical Depression Nineteen-E, and a low south of southern Mexico at 70% odds through seven days) against a suppressed Atlantic with no development expected. The North American Monsoon is in its fading late-season phase but still contributes the Pacific-and-Gulf moisture overlap feeding the Southwest-to-South-Central and now South/Southeast rains. Severe-weather season is settling into its expected late-season, ridge-periphery mode rather than a deep-Plains outbreak, consistent with today's capped, heavy-rain-dominated setup. Teleconnection indices carry only cross-check weight and lag the height field: the modestly negative PNA (-0.34) is consistent with the absence of a durable western ridge at the moment, which squares with the 6-10 day flattening more than the week-2 rebuild; the sharply negative NAO (7-day mean near -1.01) fits the high-latitude blocking tendency over Hudson Bay/the Arctic. These should not be leaned on hard, and the templated "summer" framing in the index notes should be read as October reality.
