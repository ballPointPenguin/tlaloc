Synoptic Picture

Split flow sets in: a deepening West Coast upper low, a stout central heat ridge, and an eastern cutoff driving Great Lakes-to-Northeast severe — with Edouard's remnants soaking east Texas

North America has reorganized into a three-part, split-flow longwave regime that is the natural next step from the blocked dipole Tlaloc has tracked all week. On the west side, a deep, amplified and retrogressive trough is anchored over the Pacific Northwest (heights near 552-560 dam with a cold pool collocated on the DuPage analysis), which WPC treats as a slow-moving upper low that will keep the region cool and unsettled — highs 10-15 degrees below normal — into early next week. In the middle, a stout ridge crests near 588-592 dam from the central Plains into the Great Lakes, with a separate subtropical high near 594-595 dam over the Southeast, and it is under this ridge that a dangerous late-summer heat wave is building and reloading. On the east side, the northern-stream cutoff that sat over the northern Plains yesterday has translated downstream and now shows a nearly closed, cold-core near-cutoff over the Great Lakes and Northeast (roughly 578-582 dam, neutral-to-slightly-negative tilt). That eastward migration of the closed low, and the amplification of a fresh West Coast trough behind the ridge, are the real changes on the map since yesterday.

Today's centers of action line up on the flanks of the ridge. The marquee severe threat is over the lower Great Lakes into the Northeast, where a fast-moving MCS with a mid-level shortwave is racing east-southeast toward the Mid-Atlantic coast; SPC carries a Slight Risk with damaging wind the primary threat and a few tornadoes possible along a north-south surface boundary from central NY into east-central PA, the main caveat being that morning convection and cloud cover could rob afternoon destabilization. A second Slight Risk covers Montana and the northern Rockies/High Plains for high-based storms and damaging gusts. Edouard, now a tropical depression over east Texas, is the heavy-rain story: it lifts north and weakens today under a Moderate Risk (3/4) for excessive rainfall and localized flash flooding, with a frontal connection funneling moisture toward the Ohio Valley. Monsoonal moisture supports additional flash-flood risk in the Southwest, and dangerous heat expands across the Middle Mississippi, Ohio, and Tennessee valleys and Mid-Atlantic through Friday.

The upper-level structure ties this together. The broad red-orange on the Air Mass RGB across the central and southern U.S. is warm, dry, low-PV air under the ridge — the heat and convective suppression there come from subsidence, warming and capping, not from any surface high — while a sharp orange-red streamer along the northwestern flank of the ridge marks the jet corridor separating that dry air from the moist Pacific/Canadian airmass. Impulses riding that corridor and the flow around the eastern cutoff are what fire the Great Lakes/Northeast and northern-Rockies storms. On the regime question, the ensemble pair is coherent and repeats yesterday's read: the 6-10 day (Sep 7-11) keeps the western ridge modest but present (+3 to +4 dam, zero line near 110W) with a deep low over Hudson Bay, while the 8-14 day (Sep 9-15) shows the ridge retreating geographically southward — the 588 dam contour sliding to the southern tier and northern Mexico with nearly zonal CONUS flow — beneath a deep polar low over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. So the high-latitude block has already flipped to a deep polar vortex-like low, and the ridge is contracting toward the Southwest rather than collapsing; the near-term central heat is durable and reloads in place. The one genuine uncertainty, flagged by WPC, is whether the Pacific Northwest upper low reinforces itself or advances east and begins suppressing the central ridge sooner — guidance diverges on that timing.

Climate Context

This is peak-season North America running close to script. The ONI table shows ENSO having warmed steadily from weak La Niña in late 2025 through neutral in early 2026 and into weak-to-moderate El Niño territory by mid-2026 (anomalies reaching roughly +0.95 to +1.39), and that warm eastern-Pacific state fits today's tropics precisely: a busy East Pacific with three named systems and another disturbance flagged for possible weekend development against a quiet Atlantic where, at the early-September climatological hurricane-season peak, only Edouard's inland remnant carries interest despite scattered Caribbean/Gulf convection on satellite. The North American Monsoon remains active, feeding daily Southwest and high-terrain convection with a possible tropical-moisture boost from Marie. Severe weather is expressing itself in the expected late-summer mode — ridge-periphery, northern-stream and frontal storms across the Great Lakes, Northeast and northern High Plains rather than deep-Plains outbreaks — and the dominant climate signal on the board remains the long-duration central-U.S. heat wave. Teleconnection indices (negative NAO around -0.90 and slightly negative PNA) are directionally consistent with eastern cutoff retention and a fading western ridge, but as weak summer signals they are used only as a lagging cross-check on the height and PV fields, not as the basis for the story.
