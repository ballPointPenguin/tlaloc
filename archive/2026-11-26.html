Synoptic Picture

Thanksgiving coastal storm lashes Mid-Atlantic as eastern cutoff gives way to a milder Pacific pattern

The Thanksgiving coastal storm we have tracked all week is now at its peak. At 09Z a roughly 998 mb low sat just off the Carolina/Mid-Atlantic coast, and SPC expects it to reach the Chesapeake by late afternoon. The tight gradient from the Carolinas to New England means a windy, wet holiday. WPC has a Slight excessive-rain risk over Delmarva and adjacent New Jersey, inside a Marginal strip from coastal North Carolina to Maine. The severe threat is minor: a low-confidence Marginal risk for eastern North Carolina and southeast Virginia, where about 600 J/kg of elevated CAPE could support an isolated damaging gust or a brief tornado. On the cold side, snow fell overnight at 0.5-1 inch per hour from St. Louis toward Farmington, MO, ending around 13Z as a warm layer moved in aloft. A 1034 mb high over the northern Plains holds teens and 20s across the northern tier. There is no tropical threat.

Aloft, a closed low of about 560 dam sits over the lower Mississippi Valley, cut off from the westerlies. A 75-85 kt jet runs along the Gulf Coast and wraps north over the Carolinas. Air at 500 mb is minus 27 to minus 35 C over the northern Plains and Great Lakes. Satellite shows dry upper-level air curling around the low's southern side and a sharp convective line along the cold front from off the Carolinas to the Bahamas. The strong western ridge sits near 120-130 W, well west of the low rather than poleward of it, so this is a ridge-cutoff pair, not a Rex block. That pair is already breaking down: a 150+ kt jet at 250 mb is pushing into the Pacific Northwest ahead of a 988 mb northeast Pacific low.

The storm reaches the Canadian Maritimes by Sunday. Damaging gusts, coastal flooding and outages continue in the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic until the gradient relaxes Sunday. A Northern Rockies shortwave drops into the Plains Saturday and spins up a Mississippi Valley low, bringing showers and storms to the lower Mississippi Valley and Southeast on Sunday. The West turns wet: rain Sunday could flood parts of the northern California coast, and a deeper system Tuesday brings heavy rain along the whole California coast and heavy Sierra snow Tuesday night. This is a real regime change, not a brief flattening. The CPC 6-10 day chart has no positive height anomaly left over the West, with a Bering/Alaska trough of about -12 to -16 dam dominating instead. At 8-14 days the trough sits over Alaska and the Yukon, a ridge covers the Southeast and Gulf, and northern-tier flow is fast and zonal. CPC favors above-normal temperatures across the entire lower 48 for December 1-5, peaking at 80-90% around Wisconsin. Models disagree on how deep the day-7 western trough gets.

Climate Context

A very strong El Niño shapes the season. The Oceanic Niño Index reached +2.46 for August-October, though it is now rising more slowly, about 0.3 per season. The coming pattern fits El Niño only in part. A deep Bering/Alaska trough, a strong Pacific jet and repeated storms into California are classic El Niño winter traits. The Southeast ridge and widespread warmth in the extended outlooks are less typical, so one week's pattern should not be pinned on ENSO alone. Hurricane season ends November 30 quietly: NHC expects no Atlantic formation in the next seven days and gives central Pacific disturbance CP96 only a 10 percent chance. CPC's teleconnection indices have not been updated in nearly a month and were left out.
