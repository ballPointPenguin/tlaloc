Synoptic Picture

Western upper low ejects into Canada, firing northern-Plains storms while the southern heat dome digs in and the meridional regime regenerates poleward rather than flattening

The organizing story is the same three-part arrangement Tlaloc has tracked all week, but with the western piece now on the move. The deep, positively tilted longwave trough that had been anchored offshore has closed off near the British Columbia/Washington border and is ejecting northeast; its parent surface low is tracking into southern Saskatchewan, dragging a cold front down into the northern Plains and upper Midwest. That leaves the day's dividing line running from the Canadian Prairies into the Dakotas. To its south, the amplified central ridge (heights near 594 dam over Oklahoma/Kansas) keeps the south-central US baking, and along the Gulf and Southeast a moist tropical airmass sits under a stalled, weak front. In short: the western trough is finally lifting out rather than sitting still, but the southern heat and the Southeast moisture regime are unmoved.

Today's centers of action are all on the ridge periphery, not in the deep Plains. The best-organized severe threat is the SPC slight-risk corridor across the north-central and northeast Plains, where afternoon redevelopment along a dryline-like surface trough and pre-frontal trough favors supercells with large hail as the primary hazard; a secondary tornado risk is flagged over southeast South Dakota where low-level shear is strongest. Heavy rain is the co-threat on the trough's cool side, with a slight excessive-rainfall risk in northwestern Montana today and the axis shifting to Iowa Tuesday into early Wednesday as the front advances. Along the central Gulf Coast, a hot, moist airmass ahead of the weak front supports intense pulse storms with damaging downbursts through the afternoon. Under the ridge, the durable signal persists — heat indices of 105-110 degrees and record-warm overnight lows across the midsection — while the West runs 10-25 degrees below normal with freezing mornings possible at higher elevations behind the departing low. Offshore, the East Pacific remains busy while the Atlantic stays quiet.

Aloft, the 500 mb pattern remains firmly meridional. The tight height gradient on the northern flank of the western trough, from the Pacific Northwest into the northern Rockies, marks a concentrated 70-100 kt jet corridor — that is the dynamical support ejecting the surface low into Canada and driving the northern-Plains convection. The Air Mass RGB shows a broad red-orange dry swath over the central and southern Plains, low-PV subsidence air under the late-summer ridge (not a stratospheric intrusion given its size), which is the real reason the midsection heat holds — midlevel warming, subsidence, and capping — rather than any modest surface high. The more compact, cyclonically curved dry streamer over the southern Rockies and northern Mexico is a more credible stratospheric-intrusion signature, wrapping a weak trough/cutoff in that corner. The key evolution question is whether this meridional regime is breaking down, and the ensemble pair says no: at 6-10 days the positive height anomaly has relinquished its mid-latitude western grip (near-zero to slightly negative across the western CONUS) and consolidated poleward into a strong ridge over the Canadian Arctic, but by 8-14 days a positive anomaly rebuilds over western Canada and the northern Plains and the western CONUS trends weakly positive again. That is not a ridge that keeps shrinking; it is a meridional regime that regenerates, with recurring Northwest troughs punching through — the WPC extended discussion has exactly this, another closed low digging toward British Columbia by next weekend. Both the 6-10 and 8-14 charts keep a positive anomaly over the Southeast, and both keep the amplitude high. This breaks somewhat from yesterday's read of a collapsing high-latitude ridge: today's guidance amplifies the Arctic ridge instead. The takeaway is geographic relocation and poleward amplification — the ridge core sitting north and its heat anchor clinging to the southern tier — not a transition to zonal flow.
