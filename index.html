Today’s Synoptic Picture

A blocked summer pattern: twin heat domes flank a slow-moving front firing pulse storms down the mid-continent

The single story today is blocking. A high-amplitude longwave ridge over the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia (heights topping 5920 dm) and a second ridge along the Atlantic Seaboard bracket a weak, flat, nearly neutral-to-positively-tilted trough across the central and southern Plains. With slack, difluent flow over the interior and only embedded shortwave vorticity to work with, this is a low-forcing, diurnally driven regime — a "ring of fire" arrangement in which the real weather lives on the ridge flanks and along the boundary threading between them. Slow feature progression means today's threats are largely repeats of yesterday's and previews of tomorrow's.

There are two centers of action. First, the convective and flash-flood axis: a southward-advancing front and an anomalous moisture plume (Gulf high pumping upper-60s to low-70s dewpoints into the Mississippi and Tennessee valleys) focus scattered to numerous storms from the Ozarks through the Ohio and Tennessee valleys today, with SPC carrying a Slight Risk and overnight Severe Thunderstorm Watch 480 and its follow-on mesoscale discussion showing the downburst-dominated character of this airmass. That threat rotates southeast Sunday as a weak surface low waves along the front, shifting the severe risk into South Carolina and east Georgia and the flash-flood concern into the southern Appalachians — high-PW, weakly sheared, wet-microburst storms rather than organized supercells. A vigorous, cold-topped convective tower is already visible on satellite over the Virginia–North Carolina coast along the baroclinic boundary near the offshore low. Second, dangerous heat on both flanks: heat indices above 105 in the Southeast under the Atlantic ridge, and a more anomalous, record-challenging heat event building under the Northwest ridge across the Intermountain West and northern Plains, with triple digits and departures 15-25 degrees above normal — Montana and Utah may flirt with all-time records Sunday before a cold front nudges relief into Montana late Sunday into Monday. Southern Arizona rounds out the map with monsoon-driven, DCAPE-fueled downburst storms spreading off the terrain into the deserts.

The upper pattern explains all of it. The Air Mass RGB is textbook: deep orange-red, dry, high-PV stratospheric air has been drawn down beneath the ridging across the northern and central Plains — the same subsidence that anchors the heat dome — while a sharp airmass boundary from the central Great Lakes southwest to the Tennessee Valley marks the jet axis and the abrupt handoff to green, deep tropical moisture over the Gulf and Southeast. That jet-axis boundary is precisely where the storms are firing. The strongest synoptic engine, a 115-knot jet streak, is bottled up on the northern flank of the Northwest ridge, well removed from the convective corridor, which is why the mid-continent storms stay pulsy and cold-pool/outflow-driven rather than well-organized. As the ridge builds northeast into the northern Plains through Sunday, expect the heat to expand northward and the convective focus to stay pinned to the slowly sagging front.

Seasonally this is a classic mid-July setup. The North American Monsoon is in season and directly responsible for the southern Arizona storm threat. Severe weather has settled into its summer pulse mode — downburst-and-flash-flood driven along slow boundaries rather than the organized supercell regimes of spring.
