Today’s Synoptic Picture

A blocked, high-amplitude June pattern parks a multi-day severe weather machine over the Upper Midwest

The organizing story today is a high-amplitude, partially blocked flow that has wedged the action into a single corridor across the nation's midsection. A deep western trough digging from the Pacific Northwest into the Great Basin and Desert Southwest is feeding disturbances eastward, while a cutoff low parked off the Georgia-South Carolina coast anchors the southern end of the pattern and keeps it from progressing. Between them, a ridge over the northern Plains and a sharp jet-driven airmass boundary set the stage. The result is a classic late-spring severe-weather setup that is already underway and will repeat tomorrow rather than sweep through and clear.

The center of action is unambiguous: the Upper Mississippi Valley and Great Lakes. At the surface, a complex low cluster over the Upper Midwest (993-1000 mb) anchors a cold front trailing southwestward into the Plains and a warm front draped east into the Ohio Valley, with the tightest gradient and strongest winds between that low and the Plains high. A secondary low over northern Texas pumps Gulf moisture northward, pooling upper-60s to low-70s dewpoints from Iowa down through Arkansas. SPC carries an Enhanced Risk today for the Upper Mississippi Valley and Midwest, with a morning QLCS line and afternoon supercells along an instability axis from northern Missouri into eastern Iowa posing the main tornado and very-large-hail threat. The Day 2 outlook confirms this is not a one-off: another Enhanced Risk is hoisted for Thursday from northeast Missouri through southern Wisconsin, Illinois, and into lower Michigan, with an early MCS expected to grow upscale and carry a strong-tornado and 75-mph-plus wind threat through the day. Secondary, lower-end threats cover the Mid-Atlantic/Appalachians, the southern Plains along the trailing front, and Florida, and WPC overlays a Slight Risk for excessive rainfall both days.

Aloft, the surface story is being driven by an unseasonably strong mid-level jet (60-80+ kt) and shortwave energy ejecting out of the western trough. The GOES-19 Air Mass RGB makes the dynamics visible: a vivid stratospheric dry intrusion of high-PV air sprawls from Montana and Wyoming down through Colorado and Kansas, and the sharpest airmass gradient — the implied jet axis — runs from the northern Plains southeast through the Ohio Valley into the Carolinas, exactly where the deep convection over southern Minnesota and Wisconsin is firing. The deep-layer shear from that jet, overlaid on a moisture-rich warm sector, is what upgrades ordinary June storms into organized, long-lived supercells and bowing MCSs. One note of caution: the 500 mb analysis describes a negatively tilted western trough while the satellite interpretation reads it as positively tilted; the precise tilt aside, both agree on strong forcing on the downstream side and a jet positioned to keep the severe corridor active through Thursday. The cutoff low in the Southeast, meanwhile, keeps that region slow and convectively messy rather than swept clean.

Climate Context

Seasonally this is peak severe-weather season across the central U.S., and today's setup is a textbook June example, with the added wrinkle of an unusually strong mid-level jet for the date driving the threat. The ENSO background is a transition state: the Oceanic Niño Index shows the tropical Pacific having bottomed out at its coldest anomaly (about 0.55°C below normal in OND 2025) and then warming through spring 2026 to roughly 0.48°C above normal by MAM, indicating a swing from a cool/La Nina-leaning regime toward weak El Nino conditions over the coming months. On the tropical side, the Atlantic basin is quiet with no development expected over the next seven days even as the loosely organized cluster in the Bay of Campeche bears watching; the official outlook keeps it sub-threshold. The East Pacific is the more active basin, with Tropical Storm Cristina spinning just south of El Salvador. Atlantic hurricane season is young (it began June 1), and today's quiet basin is consistent with a typically slow early-June start.
