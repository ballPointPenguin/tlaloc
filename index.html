The amplified regime reorganizes: a retrograding northern cutoff and a strong Atlantic ridge flank a Southwest heat dome now poised to slide north and east onto the Plains

The multi-week amplified regime is still with us but has rearranged its furniture. Today's 500 mb map reads as a three-part, decidedly non-progressive pattern: the western/Southwest ridge that has anchored the heat wave, a broad cutoff low near 552 dam over the northern Plains and Canadian border, and a strong ridge over the eastern seaboard and western Atlantic (588-594 dam) with an embedded anticyclone near the mid-Atlantic and a partially detached subtropical low off the Southeast coast. The 500 mb summary flirts with a Rex-block label there, but because the anticyclone sits east of the low rather than plainly poleward of it, this is better called an amplified eastern ridge with a subtropical low tucked to its south-southeast — not a true Rex geometry. The central cutoff is the successor to yesterday's slow Great Lakes low, now consolidated farther northwest and cut off enough that it should pivot and retrograde rather than translate cleanly downstream. The centers of action ring the pattern, as they have all week. The northern cutoff and its front are the dynamic engine: the Air Mass RGB shows a compact cyclonic cloud curl with cold, deep convective tops over the upper Great Lakes, SPC carries a Slight Risk for severe storms across the Upper Mississippi Valley, and WPC shifts a heavy-rain and excessive-rainfall corridor into the Upper Great Lakes and Central Plains Wednesday into Thursday as the front pushes east. The North American Monsoon is the second focus, with SPC's higher-confidence severe threat actually in southeast Arizona — scattered 60-80 mph microburst wind gusts off high-terrain storms with steep lapse rates and strong evaporative cooling — plus a Slight Risk excessive-rainfall/flash-flood threat across the Southwest. Aloft the surface story holds together. The jet streak wrapping the southern and eastern flank of the northern cutoff (winds above 70 kt) supplies the forcing for that front, and the sharp Air Mass boundary running from the upper Great Lakes southwest through the Ohio Valley into the Carolinas marks the baroclinic zone and implied jet corridor separating green tropical air to the southeast from dry orange air to the northwest. That broad orange-red over the central and southern Plains and the mid-South is warm, dry, subsident mid- and upper-tropospheric air under the ridge, not a stratospheric intrusion — the one clean high-PV signature on the imagery is the compact filament wrapping a closed low over the Canadian Maritimes and western Atlantic, a separate downstream feature. On the regime question, the ensemble pair tells a clearer story than a shortwave-by-shortwave read would: the 6-10 day (Aug 9-13) still keeps the positive-height core over western North America, with a roughly +90 m center over the north-central Pacific into BC/Alberta and the zero-anomaly line near the 100th meridian; but by the 8-14 day (Aug 11-17) the ridge core has relocated east onto the central and northern Plains (588 dam near Nebraska-Kansas) while the Pacific Northwest and western Canada slide under a negative anomaly. WPC's extended discussion bridges the two cleanly: the Southwest ridge expands north and east this weekend, driving heat into the Intermountain West and High Plains, then a Northwest trough early next week finally weakens the Southwest ridge as the warmth migrates east toward the Mississippi Valley. So the amplification is neither collapsing nor merely flattening — it is relocating north and east onto the Plains while the West cools. Worth flagging that this is a meaningful update from yesterday's guidance, which had the ridge collapsing toward an unusual high-latitude Arctic core; today's runs instead keep a robust CONUS ridge, just shifted eastward.
