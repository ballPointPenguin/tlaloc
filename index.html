Today’s Synoptic Picture

A progressive northern-stream trough drags a slow front across the East, wringing out flooding rains and Plains hail while the subtropical ridge bakes the South and Southwest

The organizing story today is a summertime split between motion and stagnation. A deep but positively tilted longwave trough sits over the northern Great Plains and upper Midwest (core near 564 dam along the Montana-Canada border), and it is the only real engine of forcing on the map. It is dragging a slow-moving cold front southeast and eroding last week's heat, while everything from the southern Plains to the Southeast is locked under a classic 590-dam subtropical ridge. Between the two, the flow is progressive and modest in amplitude — no closed low, no true blocking — which is why the WPC calls this a multi-day, multi-round frontal story rather than a single big event.

Today's centers of action are clustered along that frontal system. The sharpest severe threat is over the eastern Dakotas into western Minnesota, where a shortwave and cold front tap MLCAPE of 2000-3000 J/kg: expect early supercells with hail potentially topping two inches before storms grow upscale into a wind-dominant line. The higher-impact hazard for many, though, is water: WPC hangs a Moderate Risk of excessive rainfall over southern New England beneath a quasi-stationary front (urban and small-stream flash flooding likely), with Slight Risk flood threats over the Mid-Atlantic/western Pennsylvania today and the Upper Midwest/northern Plains midweek. Diurnal convection continues along the Gulf Coast and Florida, and satellite shows an organized cluster over the Bay of Campeche — but NHC expects no tropical development anywhere in the Atlantic or East Pacific over the next seven days, so this is heavy rain, not a cyclone.

The upper-level structure ties it together and explains the persistence. That dry, high-PV air overlays the tropical green airmass flooding the Gulf Coast, Ohio Valley, and Mid-Atlantic — the recipe for steep lapse rates over moist, unstable air and the reason storms are firing where they are. In short, the threat migrates and re-fires rather than shutting off, and a quasi-stationary circulation over the Mid South bears watching as a lingering focus later this week.

Seasonally this is peak severe-and-flood-season Plains and mid-summer for the tropics, and today fits that mold: the northern-stream trough and its EML-fed supercell corridor are textbook early-July, while a quiet NHC outlook is unremarkable for a hurricane season that has not yet reached its August-September climatological ramp.
