Today’s Synoptic Picture

A blocked, amplified pattern pins the action along a jet arcing from the Northern Plains to the Mid-Atlantic

Today's map is organized by a single dominant contrast: a deep, negatively tilted longwave trough anchored over the northern Rockies and Canadian border squares off against a stout subtropical ridge inflating heights (588-594 dm) from California and the Great Basin across Texas and the Gulf Coast. That ridge is acting as a block, so the trough is not progressing — instead it is parking an active, slow-moving weather pattern along the boundary between the two airmasses. The Air Mass RGB draws that boundary vividly: a deep red-orange stratospheric/PV intrusion sweeps from the Pacific Northwest across the northern Rockies into the Upper Midwest, with the jet axis riding its southern edge from Montana through Iowa toward the Ohio Valley, right where dry descending air gives way to a green tropical airmass. Everything of consequence is happening along and south of that seam.

There are really two centers of action today, both children of that jet. In the Northern Plains (northern North Dakota), storms firing ahead of the embedded shortwave have steep lapse rates and adequate shear for organized cells with large hail and damaging gusts — the more dynamically supported of the two threats. Along the Mid-Atlantic, from eastern Pennsylvania and New Jersey through the Carolina Piedmont, weak-shear but moisture-laden convection is the concern, with WPC hoisting a Moderate Risk for excessive rainfall and localized flash flooding and SPC a Slight Risk for scattered wind damage; lee troughing east of the Blue Ridge is the local focus. Secondary, more diurnal and outflow-driven storms round out the picture over west Texas and across the Gulf Coast, Lower Mississippi Valley, and Florida, where deep tropical moisture (Gulf dewpoints in the upper 70s) supports water-loaded downburst potential but little organization.

The upper pattern is the reason this matters going forward. The negative tilt maximizes cyclonic forcing on the trough's downstream side, and the 40-50 kt jet streak through the Dakotas is the engine for the better-organized Plains storms. But the blocking ridge means slow system movement rather than a clean sweep, favoring repeated convection and a lingering flash-flood signal, especially in the Mid-Atlantic where the front stalls and weak flow keeps storms slow. The SPC Day 2 outlook signals the overall severe threat winding down to Marginal on Monday for the eastern Dakotas into northern Minnesota and again the Mid-Atlantic, as the parent trough lifts into Ontario and Hudson Bay and ridging rebuilds northward — a de-amplification that should gradually relax the pattern early next week. Meanwhile the Desert Southwest and Baja sit under reddish subsidence and elevated PV beneath the ridge, suppressing convection there for now.
