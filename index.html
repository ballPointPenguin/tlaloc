Today’s Synoptic Picture

Two tropical systems bracket the continent while the amplified dipole reshuffles — Isaias aims at the Gulf Coast, a cutoff low drops into the Northwest, and the ensembles keep contracting the ridge south rather than reloading it

The heaviest dynamical load on the continent-scale map today sits in the tropics, as it did yesterday, but the mid-latitude flow underneath it has reshuffled. At the surface, Hurricane Isaias is the dominant feature: a 970 mb, 70-knot center in the Gulf, which the Air Mass RGB resolves as a compact bright-white core over or just inland of the Louisiana-Mississippi coast beneath a broad cold-cloud canopy fanning northeast into the Carolinas and Mid-Atlantic. WPC has it intensifying and making landfall Friday evening from the Florida Panhandle to southern Mississippi, then turning inland across central Alabama and interacting with a frontal boundary — a classic tropical-plus-front coupling that expands the wind and rain field well inland. Behind and above it, the week's amplified pattern has rotated into a new configuration: the western ridge Tlaloc tracked all week has been replaced by a deep, genuinely cutoff low over the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia (closed contours near or below 552 dam), while the 500 mb ridge has jumped east to sit over the northern Plains and upper Midwest with heights near an unusually high 594 dam and a warm core beneath the axis. This is the inland-trough/ending-Western-heat transition the last several Tlaloc analyses forecast, now unambiguously underway — the October California heatwave moderates Friday as the trough's colder air arrives, with high-elevation wet snow possible by Saturday along the Montana-Washington border.

Three focal points follow. First, Isaias and its moisture plume: a moderate excessive-rainfall threat from southern Alabama to the western Florida Peninsula Friday into Saturday, with 5-plus inches near the coast, and — per the extended guidance — the remnant circulation lifting northeast to spread heavy rain and isolated flash flooding into the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast through Sunday, especially where it tangles with frontal boundaries. Second, the western cutoff: it will phase with the remnants of East Pacific Rachel to throw widespread rain across the Southwest, Great Basin, Rockies and eventually the northern Plains this weekend into Monday, with accumulating high-elevation snow over the northern Rockies and a reinforcing shot of Simon's moisture arriving behind it early next week. Third, a broad anticyclonic corridor across the northern tier at the surface — stacked 1022-1026 mb highs from the Great Basin through the Canadian Prairies into the upper Midwest — anchoring dry, settled weather under the ridge while a 1013 mb Plains low drags a cold front through the mid-Mississippi and Tennessee valleys.

Upstairs the structure is coherent. The flow is high-amplitude and nearly meridional, with a tight gradient between the Northwest cutoff and the ridge crest implying a strong jet streak, better than 105 knots, arcing west-northwest to east-southeast across the northern tier. The DuPage analysis is careful, and so should we be: this is a quasi-stationary western-interior ridge flanked by semi-isolated lows, not a Rex block, since the Northwest low is poleward of the ridge only in a zonal sense rather than stacked above a cutoff at the same longitude. The Air Mass RGB ties the eastern half together: a sharp, saturated orange-red dry streamer wraps cyclonically around Isaias's northwestern flank — a stratospheric dry intrusion drawn down the poleward-cyclonic side of the trough — while the cleanest airmass boundary, and thus the implied jet corridor, runs along the northeastern edge of the tropical shield from the Tennessee Valley to the Mid-Atlantic, where tropical green gives way abruptly to dry high-PV air over the Ohio Valley and Great Lakes. Over western Canada, the surface signature of the Northwest cutoff is a vigorous 964 hPa low near the Gulf of Alaska, its eastern flank packing a strong gradient against coastal British Columbia and driving brisk northwesterly flow down the Prairie corridor behind the front.

On the regime question, the two ensemble height outlooks point the same way and settle it cleanly. The northern-Plains ridge dominating today's analysis is transient — neither the 6-10 nor the 8-14 day mean reloads a ridge over the northern tier. Both are organized around a deep closed low and strong negative height anomaly over the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, a displaced polar-vortex lobe rather than the high-latitude block the indices hint at. The 6-10 day (valid Oct 13-17) goes nearly zonal and progressive across the CONUS with the western positive anomaly retreated north to near the Pacific coast and near-zero to weakly negative values over the lower 48; the 8-14 day (valid Oct 15-21) then re-amplifies but with the ridge pushed into the southern tier — highest heights of 576-582 dam over the Gulf Coast states and a +3 to +6 dam anomaly across the south. Side by side, that is a ridge contracting and relocating south, not collapsing and not rebuilding in place: the amplified blocked dipole is genuinely giving way to a more progressive flow with a deep Canadian trough and a southern-CONUS ridge. The CPC 6-10 temperature outlook — 90-100% above normal on the Gulf Coast, a weak 33-40% cool lean confined to the Northeast — is the fingerprint of that retreated-but-potent southern ridge, not a contradiction. One honest caveat carried over from yesterday: the negative AO (-0.26) and NAO (-0.78) would nominally argue for high-latitude blocking, yet the height means show an Arctic vortex instead. The indices lag the height field and carry only cross-check weight, and here the height and PV fields disagree with them, so defer to the heights.
